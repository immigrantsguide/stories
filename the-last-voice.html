AN ORIGINAL CRIME THRILLER · PART ONE

THE LAST VOICE

She wanted one last hug. He wanted one more chance. Someone else bought her final words.

Created by Vijay Krishna


Hyderabad was a city that rarely stopped moving. In L.B. Nagar, buses pushed through traffic, vendors called out to passing customers, ambulances searched for space, and families hurried through hospital doors carrying the one thing they could not afford to lose: hope.


Ravi and Sruthi lived in a modest apartment not far from the busy roads. They were not wealthy. Their home was small, their furniture ordinary, and their plans often limited by monthly expenses. But their life had been full of little rituals that belonged only to them.


When Ravi returned from work, he would call, “Sruthi! Where is my queen?”

From the kitchen she would answer, “Your queen is working. If the king wants dinner, the king can come and help.”

He would appear at the kitchen door, steal a piece of food, and pretend not to notice her annoyed expression. She would threaten to stop cooking for him. He would promise to order food the next day. Both knew neither of them meant it.


They had built their happiness from small things: tea shared after a tiring day, a hand held while crossing a road, a favourite sweet kept aside, and the quiet comfort of knowing someone was waiting at home. Sometimes, when Ravi fell asleep, his fingers would find Sruthi’s hand without him knowing. She loved that more than any expensive gift.


One night, she had asked him what he wanted when they grew old.

“A house with a garden,” Ravi had said.

“You don't even water the one plant we have,” she replied.

“Then I'll hire a gardener.”

“And what will I do?”

“You'll sit beside me and complain about the gardener.”

She had rested her head on his shoulder. “I don't need a big house, Ravi. I need a small table, two chairs, and you sitting opposite me.”

He had taken her hand. “Nothing in this world is more important than you.”


But ordinary life can wear people down. Work pressure, money worries and a misunderstanding that neither was willing to put aside turned a small argument into a long silence. Ravi became angry. Sruthi tried to explain, then tried to give him space. Fifteen days passed. They still lived under the same roof, but their words had become short and careful.


Sruthi missed him. She missed the man who used to steal food from her plate and call her queen. She decided that she would stop waiting for the perfect moment to make peace. She would make one good dinner, write a note, and ask him to begin again.


That morning, Ravi was getting ready for work. Sruthi placed his bag near the door and offered him coffee.

“Coffee?”

“I'm getting late,” he replied without looking at her.

She nodded. She did not argue. When he left, she stood for a moment near the doorway, then went to the kitchen.


She prepared his favourite lunch. She packed rice, the curry he always asked for, and a sweet she knew he liked. She arranged everything carefully in his lunchbox. In the first compartment she placed a folded note addressed to him. Before leaving, she looked at their wedding photograph and whispered, “Tonight, let's stop being strangers.”


She packed the lunchbox, took her scooter keys and went out. On the way, she dropped Ravi at his office. She gave him the lunchbox and reminded him to eat. He accepted it without saying much. She waited a second, hoping he might look at her or say something kind. He had already turned towards the office entrance.


Sruthi rode home alone.


Near a bus stop in L.B. Nagar, a little girl was standing with her mother. Her father had walked a short distance away to buy ice cream. The girl held a bright red balloon by its string. A gust of wind pulled it from her fingers. The balloon rose above the bus shelter and drifted towards the road.


“My balloon!” the girl cried. She ran after it.

Her mother shouted, “Baby, stop! Come back!”

The father turned from the ice-cream stall. He saw his daughter moving towards traffic and dropped what he was holding. He ran, calling her name.


A bus was approaching. Its driver saw the child and braked hard. The mother ran after her, frightened and unable to reach her. The little girl looked back at her parents, lost her balance and fell onto the road. For one terrible moment, she was directly in the path of the bus.


Her father reached her just in time. He pulled her close and threw himself backwards towards the roadside. The bus passed dangerously close. The girl was safe. Her father held her against his chest, shaking.

“Daddy, I want to go home,” she whispered.

“We are going home, baby. I'm here.”

The mother reached them and wrapped both of them in her arms. She kept kissing her daughter's forehead, crying and asking why she had run. The father could not answer. He simply held his child tighter.


Sruthi had stopped her scooter nearby. She watched the family reunite. Her eyes filled with relief. She was glad the little girl was safe. She waited until the family had moved away from the road, then started home again.


She did not know that the same road was about to take her away from the people she loved.


A black BMW came from the wrong side at dangerous speed. Sruthi saw it too late. She tried to turn away, but there was nowhere safe to go. The car struck her scooter. The impact threw her onto the windshield, which shattered. She fell onto the road. Two motorcycles approaching behind could not stop in time; the riders lost control and collided in the confusion. Sruthi suffered catastrophic injuries, including severe head trauma, internal bleeding and deep wounds from the broken glass.


The BMW stopped. The driver got out and looked at the injured woman. People were already running towards the accident. Someone shouted at him to help. Instead, he returned to the car and drove away.


Sruthi's phone lay near the roadside. Its screen lit up. Ravi was calling. The call stopped. A moment later, it rang again. A passer-by answered.

“Hello? Are you her husband? Your wife has met with a serious accident. We are taking her to the hospital. Please come immediately.”


At his office, Ravi had seen Sruthi's calls earlier. He was still hurt and angry. He had put his phone on silent after deciding he did not want another conversation that morning. He did not know that every unanswered call was carrying the last chance he would have to hear her voice while she was still alive.


That evening, he returned home and found the house locked. He opened the door and called her name. There was no answer. He checked the kitchen, bedroom and balcony. Her scooter was missing. He sat down and looked at his phone. There were many missed calls from an unfamiliar number.


He called back.

“Good evening. Emergency department.”

“This number called me. I'm Ravi. Who called?”

“Sir, are you related to Sruthi?”

“I'm her husband. What happened?”

“Your wife was brought here after a road accident. Please come immediately.”

Ravi stood so quickly that his chair fell behind him. “Is she alive?”

The person on the other end asked him to come at once.


He reached the hospital in a panic. The emergency department was crowded and overwhelmed. Ambulances were arriving. A child with a fractured arm was crying in one corner. An elderly man was being moved for treatment. A nurse called for blood, another asked for a specialist, and relatives crowded around the reception desk asking for updates.


Ravi pushed towards the emergency staff, asking for Sruthi. A nurse stopped him and explained that she was in critical condition. A doctor told him that she had suffered severe head injuries and internal bleeding. They were trying to stabilise her.

“Please save her,” Ravi said. “Money is not a problem. Do whatever you need to do.”

“We need to act quickly,” the doctor replied.

Ravi watched the ICU doors close. He placed his palm against the glass and whispered, “Sruthi, I'm here. I'm sorry. I should have answered.”


The hospital was busy day and night. Patients filled the wards, relatives waited on plastic chairs, and staff moved from one emergency to another. Ravi spent hours outside the ICU. Every time a nurse came through the doors, he stood up. Every time he asked how Sruthi was, the answer was that the team was still trying.


The first bill was more than he expected. Ravi paid it. Then came another bill for additional treatment, medicines, investigations and ICU care. He emptied his savings, borrowed from friends and relatives, took a personal loan and pledged what little gold he could. He stopped thinking about the future. There was only one thing that mattered: Sruthi had to come home.


At the billing counter, he tried to understand why the amount kept rising.

“I paid yesterday,” he said. “Why is there another bill?”

“Those were the previous charges, sir. These are additional charges.”

“Please give me time. I am arranging the money.”

“We need payment against the account.”

Ravi looked towards the ICU. “My wife is fighting for her life inside. How can you talk to me as if this is just an account?”


Behind the busy public face of the hospital, a smaller group of administrators was running a criminal operation. They secretly recorded the final words of selected patients whose families had large unpaid bills. Those recordings were placed in private auctions for wealthy buyers. Some buyers wanted to possess the last words of strangers. Others wanted to suppress evidence, acquire private information or silence people who had heard something dangerous. The hospital's constant activity helped hide the operation. There were always patients to treat, bills to print, relatives to manage and emergencies to answer.


The operation was managed by an administrator named Mahesh. He kept a private catalogue of recordings, patient details, family finances and expected auction values. He spoke about grief as if it were inventory.

“People will pay more when a voice means something to them,” he told an employee. “And people who cannot pay their bills have very few choices.”


Sruthi briefly regained consciousness. A nurse asked whether she could hear her. Sruthi struggled to speak. She asked for Ravi. Then she remembered the car.

“Black BMW,” she whispered.

“Which car, ma'am?”

“Number... TS 09 EF 7070.”

The nurse leaned closer.

“Please... don't let him go. The man who hit me...”


Sruthi asked to leave a message for her husband. The hospital's recording system captured her words, but Mahesh later arranged for an edited copy to be prepared for the auction. He wanted the most emotional parts and did not want the vehicle number or anything that could identify the driver to reach the public.


In the recording, Sruthi spoke slowly, with long pauses between sentences.


“My name is Sruthi. I'm not a rich woman. I don't have a big house or expensive jewellery. I have a husband named Ravi. I think I received the biggest gift in my life when I married him.


“When we first met, he was quiet. I talked so much that he used to ask if I had a battery that never ran out. He teased me, and I pretended to be angry. But he always knew how to make me smile.


“We did not have a perfect life. We fought about money, food, work and silly things. Sometimes we hurt each other with words. But even on our worst days, I knew I had someone to come home to.


“When I was sick, he stayed awake. When he was tired, I made him tea. When he worried about work, I held his hand. We did not have everything, but we had each other.


“Do you know what I loved most about him? When he slept, he used to hold my hand without knowing. I would wake up and find his fingers holding mine. I never told him how much that meant to me.


“Today he is angry with me. We haven't spoken properly for fifteen days. I know I made mistakes. He made mistakes too. But I don't want to count them anymore. I only want to go home.


“I want to open the door and hear him call, ‘Where is my queen?’ I want to tell him not to shout because the neighbours will hear. I want to make tea. I want to sit beside him. I want to grow old with him. I want to complain about his snoring and fight over the television remote. I want to see his hair turn grey.


“I want all those ordinary things. I understand now that those ordinary things were my entire happiness.


“If I don't survive, please tell Ravi he must eat properly. He skips meals when he is worried. He doesn't take care of himself. Please tell him not to blame himself. I know he didn't answer my calls today. I know he was angry. But I know his heart. He would never knowingly hurt me.


“And if he is listening, Ravi, my love, I am sorry. I am sorry for every fight and every tear. But I am not sorry that I married you. If God gives me another life, let me find you again. Even if we live in a small house, even if we struggle for money, even if we fight every day, I will still choose you.


“I don't want another husband. I don't want another home. I only want my Ravi. If I cannot come back, please don't forget that somewhere in this world there was a woman who loved you more than her own life.


“I love you, Ravi. I love you so much.”


She paused, trying to breathe.

“Please... give him the hug I couldn't give him.”


Then came her final words, faint but clear: “Ravi... if you're listening... I saw the car. A black BMW\... number TS 09 EF 7070. Please don't let him go. And there was one more person beside the driver. I couldn't see his face clearly, but I heard his voice. It was Arjun... Mr. Vikram Varma's son...”


The recording ended. Sruthi died despite the doctors' efforts.


Ravi was told that she could not be saved. He did not accept it at first. He asked the doctor to try again, as though the right words could reverse what had happened. Then the doctor lowered his eyes, and Ravi understood. He sat outside the ICU, unable to move.


A staff member approached him with a final bill. The amount included further treatment charges and disputed additional fees. Ravi looked at the figure and said he had nothing left. He had borrowed everything he could. He had no savings, no jewellery and no one else to ask.


Then the staff member told him that the hospital had recorded Sruthi's final words. If he could not clear the outstanding amount, the recording would be auctioned.


Ravi stared at him. “You recorded my wife's last words?”

“It is part of the settlement process,” the employee replied.

“She was dying. She was asking for me. And you were recording her?”


Ravi begged them to give him the recording. He said he did not want the money, did not want compensation and did not want to make a complaint. He only wanted to hear his wife's voice. He offered to work at the hospital, to clean the floors, to do anything they asked. When they refused, he fell at the manager's feet.

“Please, sir. I couldn't answer her calls. At least let me hear what she wanted to tell me. Please don't sell the last thing she left behind.”


The manager pulled away and told security to remove him. The auction continued in a private room. Wealthy buyers sat behind closed doors while the hospital carried on treating patients outside. The recording was listed as a lot. Bids rose quickly.


A man named Vikram Varma arrived in a black Porsche. He was a powerful businessman, accustomed to having doors opened for him. He listened to a short sample and offered more than three times the outstanding bill. The room fell silent. The recording was sold to him.


Vikram took the device and left without speaking to anyone.


Ravi returned home as if he had aged years in a single day. The house was dark. On the dining table sat Sruthi's lunchbox. He had not noticed it when he left that morning. He sat down and opened the first compartment. Inside was her folded note.


“My Ravi,


“I know you are still angry with me. I don't know how many more days you are planning to punish me with your silence, but I miss you.


“I miss the Ravi who used to wait near the kitchen just to steal a bite of food. I miss the Ravi who held my hand while crossing the road, even when there were no vehicles. I miss our silly fights. I miss the way you used to call my name when you came home.


“Today I decided I would not let this silence continue. I made your favourite food. I even made that sweet you always ask me to prepare. Tonight, I want us to forget everything. I want to sit beside you, hold your hand and rest my head on your shoulder.


“I don't need expensive gifts, Ravi. I don't need a perfect husband. I just need my husband back.


“Do you remember what you told me on our wedding night? You said that no matter how difficult life became, you would never let me feel alone. I believed you. I still believe you.


“If I could choose again, among all the people in this world, I would still choose you. A thousand times. In a thousand lives. Because loving you was never a mistake. It was my favourite part of being alive.


“Your wife, your best friend, your biggest fan, Sruthi.


“P.S. Don't forget to eat your lunch. I know you skip it when you're angry.”


There was one more small note: “Tonight, no fighting. Only hugs. Deal?”


Ravi's hands began to shake. He opened the next compartment and found the sweet she had made. He remembered how she used to save the best piece for him. He pressed the note to his chest and cried. He looked at his phone and saw the missed calls again. He listened to the ringing tone, one call after another, as though he could somehow answer them now.


“Just one more call, Sruthi,” he whispered. “Please call me once more.”


He had not eaten the lunch she made. He had not read the note when she was alive. He had not answered the calls that could have been their last conversation. The food cooled on the table while the house held the silence that had destroyed them.


The next day, Ravi returned to the hospital. He confronted the staff and demanded to know who had bought the recording. When they refused to answer, his grief turned into uncontrolled rage. He smashed property, attacked staff and injured doctors and security personnel who tried to restrain him. The police were called. In the confusion, Ravi found an auction receipt and the buyer's name: Vikram Varma. He also found an address in Jubilee Hills.


Ravi went to Vikram's mansion. He fought past the security guards and entered the living room. Vikram was sitting alone, the recording device on the table. Ravi demanded to know why he had bought his wife's final words.

“I couldn't pay the bill,” Ravi said. “You paid more than three times that amount just to own her voice. What did you want? Her pain? Her crying? Her final breath?”


Vikram did not argue. He asked Ravi to listen before judging him. He played the full recording. Ravi heard Sruthi's voice, her memories, her small dreams and her final request for one last hug. He sat down and listened without moving. When the recording ended, he asked Vikram to play it again.


Vikram's eyes filled with tears. He had spent his life buying things, protecting his wealth and believing that money could solve every problem. Sruthi had possessed almost nothing by comparison, yet she had spoken about a life rich in love. He whispered, “I have spent my entire life buying things. This woman had so little, but she had everything.”


Then Ravi asked why Vikram had paid so much.

“I bought it to protect my son,” Vikram admitted.

“Your son?”

“The BMW was driven by my son, Arjun.”


Vikram explained that Arjun had called him after the accident. Rather than report the incident, he had contacted Mahesh, the hospital administrator. The hospital's secret network had helped wealthy clients suppress evidence and acquire private recordings. Vikram had used the network before to protect his son from the consequences of other incidents. He had bought Sruthi's recording because he feared it might identify Arjun.


But Sruthi had identified him anyway.


Vikram confronted Arjun at the mansion. At first, Arjun insisted that it was an accident and that he had panicked. Then Vikram asked why he had fled instead of helping Sruthi. Arjun said he had called Mahesh, who had handled the situation before. Vikram realised that this was not the first time his son had used money and influence to escape responsibility.


“How many times?” Vikram asked.

Arjun avoided his eyes.

“How many times have you done this?”

“Dad, you can manage this. You have money.”

Vikram looked at the recording device. “I spent my life making sure you never suffered. And you made another family suffer for the rest of their lives.”


Vikram contacted the police. He handed over the original recording, the BMW details, messages between Arjun and Mahesh, financial records and documents showing his own involvement in the illegal auction network. He told the officers to arrest his son and himself if the evidence supported it. He also gave Ravi the original recording so that no one could take it away from him again.


Meanwhile, the little girl's father, Suresh, had something important. He had been near the ice-cream stall when the accident happened. His delivery vehicle had a dash camera. After seeing news of the accident, he checked the footage and realised that it showed the BMW hitting Sruthi and fleeing. He took the footage to the police.


“That woman stopped her scooter when my daughter nearly got hit by a bus,” Suresh told the inspector. “She waited until my child was safe. I never got a chance to thank her.”


The footage clearly showed the BMW registration: TS 09 EF 7070. It supported Sruthi's final words and gave investigators independent evidence of the hit-and-run.


Police arrested Arjun for the fatal accident. Mahesh and other hospital employees were arrested as the investigation uncovered the illegal recording and auction operation. Vikram was also arrested for his role in protecting his son and participating in the network. Ravi was arrested for the violence at the hospital and the injuries caused during his confrontation.


Ravi and Arjun were eventually held in the same prison complex. Arjun remained arrogant. He believed his father's money and lawyers would find a way to help him. Ravi barely spoke. He spent his nights listening to Sruthi's voice through the recording device he had been permitted to keep as evidence only during supervised proceedings; he was no longer allowed to carry it freely. He kept her photograph instead.


One day, Arjun taunted him. He said he barely remembered the woman he had hit. He told Ravi that he should have answered her calls. Ravi warned him not to speak about Sruthi. Arjun repeated her name deliberately, mocking the grief that had consumed Ravi.


A violent confrontation followed. Prison officers intervened, but Arjun suffered fatal injuries. Ravi was taken away and faced a new investigation and serious charges. He had taken revenge, but it had not brought Sruthi back. Alone in his cell, he looked at her photograph and whispered, “I thought it would stop hurting. Why does it still hurt, Sruthi?”


Vikram learned what had happened. He had turned his son in because he believed the truth mattered more than protecting his family name. Now his son was dead, and Ravi's life had been destroyed further. Vikram could not undo any of it. He could only cooperate with the investigation and accept the consequences of his own choices.


But the case was not over.


At the police cybercrime office, an officer examined the hospital's seized digital archive. He found the original recording of Sruthi's final words. It was longer than the version sold at auction. The officer listened carefully. After Sruthi identified the BMW, a male voice could be heard asking whether the recording was running. Another voice replied that the number plate was clear. Someone instructed the staff to remove that section before the auction.


The officer checked the access records. Vikram's account had accessed the file, but another administrator account had also altered it. The account belonged to Mahesh. The archive showed that someone was still accessing the hospital's recordings, even after the arrests.


Then the officer heard a final fragment that had not been included in any version previously recovered.


Sruthi's voice was faint. She was trying to say something more about the person beside the driver.


“The person beside the driver... it wasn't just Arjun...”


A pause.


“It was a woman too...”


The officer replayed the fragment. He checked the original metadata. The woman's identity had been removed from every copy. The police had evidence of Arjun driving, but they now had to find out who else was in the car and why that person had been hidden.


Before the investigators could follow the new lead, Mahesh received a call from an unknown number.

“You sold Sruthi's voice,” the caller said. “But you forgot one thing. She identified the car, the driver, and the person beside him.”

Mahesh asked who was calling.

“The person who was supposed to be dead.”


The call ended. Mahesh looked towards the locked archive room. Someone stood behind its glass door, but the corridor lights failed before he could see the person's face.


At the hospital, the emergency department continued to fill with patients. Ambulances arrived. Doctors treated the injured. Families waited for news. The hospital looked as busy as ever, but the secret network had not disappeared with the arrests. Somewhere in its archive, another recording was marked active.


The file was labelled PATIENT 048. Its status read: ALIVE.


A new buyer had offered two crore rupees for it.


The person behind the request was hidden. The recording began with a woman's frightened voice asking not to be allowed to die. Then, from somewhere close to the microphone, another voice spoke.


“The next auction starts tomorrow.”


And the voice sounded familiar to Mahesh.


It belonged to someone whose death had been recorded years earlier.


Sruthi's final words had exposed a driver, a corrupt administrator and a wealthy family. But they had also uncovered a secret that reached far beyond one accident. The identity of the woman in the BMW, the real scale of the hospital's network, and the person who had returned from the dead remained unknown.


Ravi had lost the woman he loved. Vikram had lost his son. Mahesh had lost control of the secret he had protected for years. And somewhere, someone was preparing the next auction.


THE END — PART ONE

Some voices disappear. Some become evidence. And some return to destroy the people who tried to silence them.
