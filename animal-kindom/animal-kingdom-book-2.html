An Original Animal Fantasy

THE ANIMAL KINGDOM

Book Two: A New Beginning

Before the kingdom united, two warriors hated each other. Before the serpent ruled from the shadows, someone had already shown him the way.

CREATED BY VIJAY KRISHNA

The forest had survived the war.

But it had not survived the loss of its king.

Under the ancient apple tree, the animals gathered in silence. Wounded deer lay beside exhausted wolves. Young rabbits sat close to their mothers. Birds rested on the lower branches without singing. Even the wind seemed afraid to disturb the sadness that had covered the Green Crown.

At the centre of the gathering stood an old, flat stone.

It was the same stone where King Simha had sat countless evenings, listening to Bujji's funny stories about stolen fruit, broken nests, and daring escapes from angry guards.

Now the stone was empty.

Bujji sat beside it with his knees pulled against his chest. His eyes were swollen, his fur was dusty, and his hands trembled whenever he looked at the place where his best friend used to sit.

"He always told me I talked too much," Bujji whispered.

Nobody answered.

"He said that one day, my stories would become so terrible that even the snakes would leave the forest."

A painful smile crossed his face.

"He was wrong."

Bujji looked at the empty stone.

"The snakes stayed. My king didn't."

His voice broke. He covered his face and wept.

At that moment, four familiar animals came through the trees.

Puli, the fierce tiger commander.

Jaga, the silent jaguar guardian.

Mangal, the king of the mongoose kingdom.

And Chinna, the red fox whose clever eyes rarely showed what he was thinking.

They had come to honour the fallen king.

Puli lowered his head beside the stone. Jaga stood a little distance away, his tail moving restlessly. Mangal stood beside his wounded people. Chinna settled beneath a low branch, watching everyone without saying a word.

For once, the fox had nothing clever to say.

Then a small voice broke the silence.

"Bujji uncle?"

Bujji slowly lifted his head.

A little golden lion cub stood beside a young mongoose. His paws were dusty, his mane was barely beginning to grow, and his eyes carried a sadness that seemed far too heavy for someone so young.

It was Simha's son.

Since his father's death, the little lion had hardly eaten. He no longer chased butterflies or jumped over fallen branches. Whenever the other young animals invited him to play, he turned away.

The young mongoose had tried everything. He had brought him berries, challenged him to races, and even pretended to fall into a bush to make him laugh.

Nothing worked.

That afternoon, he finally brought Simha's son to Bujji.

"Uncle, he wants to know about his father," the young mongoose said.

Bujji stared at the cub.

Simha's son looked exactly like his father had looked as a child. The same golden eyes. The same small black mark near the left ear. The same expression whenever he was trying to understand something important.

Bujji's heart seemed to stop.

The little lion approached the stone.

"Did my father really save everyone?"

Bujji tried to answer, but nothing came out.

"Was he really brave?"

The monkey's eyes filled with tears.

The cub lowered his head.

"Or did he leave because I wasn't good enough to make him stay?"

A gasp passed through the animals.

Bujji moved so quickly that the cub barely saw him. He gathered Simha's son into his arms and held him tightly.

"Never say that again," Bujji whispered.

The cub began to cry.

"Then why didn't he come back?"

Bujji closed his eyes. He had no answer that could make the pain disappear.

He held the little lion closer.

"Come here, little king. Sit with me."

He settled on the old stone and placed the cub beside him.

"Tonight, I will tell you a story."

The cub wiped his eyes.

"About my father?"

Bujji nodded.

"Not just the story everyone will tell you. Not just the story about the king who fought the biggest battle in the forest. I will tell you about the little lion who once had nothing. About the friend who stole food for him. About the enemies who learned to stand together. And about the first time your father discovered what it truly meant to be brave."

Bujji gently placed a hand on the cub's head.

"But first, look at the sky."

The stars were appearing between the branches of the apple tree.

"Do you see them?"

The cub nodded.

"Those stars remind us of our ancestors. Of the ones who loved us, protected us, and made sacrifices so that we could live. They cannot walk beside us anymore, little one, but what they taught us lives on in every good thing we do."

He kissed the cub's forehead.

"Be kind. Be brave. Protect those who cannot protect themselves. And never become cruel just because the world has been cruel to you."

The cub leaned against him.

"Will you tell me everything?"

"Everything I remember."

Bujji looked up at the stars.

"And, Simha, old friend, forgive me if I cry while telling it."

The monkey took a trembling breath.

Long before Simha wore a crown, the Green Crown belonged to another lion.

His name was Maharaja Surya.

He was a mighty king, respected by elephants, feared by predators, and loved by the smallest animals in the forest. His mane shone like the last light of sunset, and his roar could silence an entire valley.

But the greatest thing about Surya was not his strength.

It was the way he loved his family.

His queen, Tara, was the only creature who could calm him when his anger rose. His young son, Simha, was the centre of his world.

One evening, the royal family travelled through a narrow valley near the western mountains.

Simha was so small that he could hide beneath his father's chest.

He spent the journey asking questions.

"Father, why do the stars appear only at night?"

"So that we remember light can exist even when we cannot see it."

"Can stars hear us?"

Surya smiled.

"Perhaps. But if you want something important heard, you should say it to someone who can answer."

Simha laughed.

Then the forest went silent.

Surya stopped walking.

The birds had vanished. The grass had stopped moving. A strange smell drifted through the valley.

Predators.

Dozens of them emerged from the rocks.

They were not ordinary hunters. Their coats were dark with mud, their claws were scarred, and their eyes burned with the hunger of animals who had spent too long following a cruel leader.

Their chief stepped forward.

"Surya," he growled. "Your kingdom ends tonight."

Tara moved closer to Simha.

Surya stood between his family and the approaching predators.

"Take one more step," the king warned, "and you will discover why my ancestors ruled this forest."

The chief laughed.

Then the attack began.

Surya charged.

He struck the first predator so hard that it crashed against a boulder. He turned, knocked another out of his path, and roared so fiercely that dust fell from the cliffs.

For a moment, it seemed impossible that anyone could defeat him.

Simha watched from behind a fallen tree, his tiny paws shaking.

His father was everywhere. One moment he was fighting three predators. The next, he was driving an entire group away from Tara.

Then Surya saw an opening.

The enemy chief had stumbled.

The king raised his enormous paw, his claws ready to end the battle.

And that was when everything changed.

A predator broke through the fighting and seized a baby lion hiding near the rocks.

The cub looked almost exactly like Simha.

Surya saw the little lion's golden face.

For the smallest moment, his heart became stronger than his instincts.

His paw hesitated.

The blow missed.

The enemy chief recovered and sprang forward, catching Surya by the throat.

"FATHER!" Simha screamed.

The sound that came from the little lion was so powerful, so full of terror and grief, that it echoed through the valley like the roar of a grown king.

Surya heard it.

He turned his head.

Across the battlefield, through the dust and blood, he saw his son standing beneath the rocks.

Their eyes met.

And Surya smiled.

It was not the smile of a defeated king. It was the smile of a father who had found what mattered most.

The enemy's claws tightened.

Surya's legs weakened.

He looked at Simha one last time.

Then the great king fell.

The battle continued around his body, but Surya never rose again.

The predators scattered only after Tara led Simha through a narrow passage between the rocks. By the time the surviving guards reached them, the king was gone.

Simha did not understand death.

He kept looking behind him.

"Mother, Father is coming, isn't he?"

Tara pressed her trembling face against his.

She could not speak.

Far above the valley, the stars began to shine.

And one small lion learned that the strongest creature in the world could still disappear.

The forest was not kind to an orphaned cub.

Tara had survived the battle, but she was badly injured. She could no longer hunt properly, and the royal guards who remained loyal to Surya were scattered across the territory.

The palace was no longer safe.

Tara took Simha away from the royal grounds and hid him near a ruined treehouse at the edge of the forest.

"Stay here," she told him one morning. "I must find food."

Simha nodded.

He waited.

The sun rose. Then it climbed high above the trees.

His stomach growled.

He tried to catch a beetle, but it escaped beneath a root. He searched for berries, but most had already been eaten by birds.

By evening, he was so hungry that he could barely stand.

That was when a voice came from above.

"Excuse me. Are you planning to eat that leaf?"

Simha looked up.

A young monkey was hanging upside down from a branch.

His name was Bujji.

"I am not eating a leaf," Simha muttered.

"Good. Because I was going to eat it."

Simha glared at him.

Bujji grinned.

"You have a very angry face for someone so small."

"Go away."

"Gladly. But first, tell me why you are sitting alone in the forest looking like a wet mango."

Simha turned his head.

Bujji's smile faded. For the first time, he noticed the cub's trembling legs and empty stomach.

"Wait here," the monkey said.

"Why?"

"Because if I tell you to follow me, you will probably refuse."

Bujji disappeared into the trees.

He returned shortly afterward carrying a stolen mango, two bananas, and a handful of nuts.

Simha stared at the food.

"Where did you get that?"

"From a very generous tree."

"Trees don't keep bananas in baskets."

"Then it was a very generous basket."

Simha took a bite.

He ate so quickly that Bujji had to pull the mango away before the cub swallowed the seed.

"Slow down! I risked my entire life for that mango."

"You stole it."

"Exactly. My criminal reputation is at stake."

For the first time since his father's death, Simha laughed.

It was a small laugh, barely more than a breath.

But Bujji heard it.

And he decided he would do whatever it took to hear it again.

From that day onward, the monkey became the cub's shadow.

When Simha was hungry, Bujji stole food.

When Simha was frightened, Bujji told jokes.

When Simha woke from nightmares, Bujji sat beside him until sunrise.

And when Simha asked whether his father would ever return, Bujji never lied.

"No," he said one night. "But we can make sure the things he taught you never disappear."

Simha rested his head against the monkey's shoulder.

"Will you stay with me?"

Bujji looked toward the stars.

"Until you become the most troublesome lion in the forest."

Simha frowned.

"I'm going to become a great king."

"Even worse," Bujji sighed. "I'll never get any sleep."

Neither of them knew that years later, the monkey would remember those words beside an empty stone.

Neither knew that one day, the little lion would save an entire kingdom.

And neither knew that the forest had already begun choosing its next king.

The Green Crown was changing.

Surya's death had left the forest weak, and every territory wanted to protect its own borders.

Among the strongest young warriors were two animals who could barely stand to be in the same clearing.

Puli, the tiger.

And Jaga, the jaguar.

Puli believed strength meant facing danger directly. Jaga believed the best warrior was the one whose enemy never saw him coming.

Their disagreements began over patrol routes.

They grew into arguments over training.

Soon, even a harmless greeting could become a challenge.

One morning, Puli found Jaga practising jumps across a narrow ravine.

"You call that training?" Puli scoffed. "A squirrel could jump farther."

Jaga landed silently behind him.

"And a rock could think faster than you."

Puli spun around.

"What did you say?"

"I said you are standing in my way."

Puli charged.

Jaga dodged.

Within seconds, the two warriors were rolling through the dust, snapping and snarling while birds fled from the trees.

Simha, still a young cub, watched from a nearby rock.

Bujji sat beside him, eating nuts.

"Should we stop them?" Simha asked.

"Absolutely," Bujji replied.

He threw a nut at Puli.

It bounced off the tiger's forehead.

Puli froze.

Jaga stopped laughing.

Bujji threw another nut.

This one struck Jaga's nose.

The jaguar slowly turned toward the monkey.

"Oh," Bujji whispered. "I have made a terrible career decision."

He ran.

Puli and Jaga chased him across the clearing, arguing about who would catch him first.

Simha watched them disappear into the trees.

Then he laughed.

Not because the fight was funny.

Because he had discovered something important.

The forest was full of powerful animals who were so busy proving they were strong that they forgot what they were supposed to protect.

That evening, Simha approached Puli and Jaga.

"I want to ask you something."

Puli lowered his head respectfully. Jaga folded his forelegs.

"Who is the strongest warrior in the forest?" Simha asked.

"Me," Puli answered immediately.

"Me," Jaga said at exactly the same time.

Simha nodded thoughtfully.

"Then why can neither of you protect the forest without fighting each other?"

Neither warrior answered.

Simha continued.

"If an enemy comes tomorrow, will you stop to decide whose claws are sharper? Or will you stand together?"

Puli looked away.

Jaga's tail became still.

The little lion bowed his head.

"My father used to say that a kingdom does not fall when its walls break. It falls when its defenders forget they belong to the same home."

Then Simha walked away.

Puli watched him go.

Jaga watched too.

Neither apologised. Neither became friends that night.

But the next morning, when Puli found Jaga waiting beside the boundary trail, the jaguar did not insult him.

He simply said, "The northern path is yours. I'll take the eastern ridge."

Puli nodded.

It was not friendship.

Not yet.

But it was the first stone on the road toward it.

As the years passed, Simha grew stronger. He learned to hunt, to fight, to listen, and to understand the needs of the animals around him.

But the forest had many secrets, and not every creature wanted peace.

Far from the Green Crown, beyond the marshes and the ruined roots of an ancient forest, a young serpent named Nagu was learning a different lesson.

Kindness was weakness.

Trust was a trap.

And power belonged to the creature clever enough to steal it.

Nagu was not the largest serpent in the marsh. He was not the fastest. But he had a talent that frightened even older snakes.

He remembered everything.

Every insult.

Every defeat.

Every secret whispered in the dark.

One night, Nagu discovered an abandoned stone chamber beneath a flooded cave. The walls were covered in ancient carvings showing a serpent wrapped around a crown.

At the centre of the chamber lay a broken pedestal.

Something was missing.

Nagu searched for days, following faded symbols through tunnels and beneath fallen trees. At last, he found a trail leading to a deep pool where the water was black even at noon.

He leaned over the edge.

"Whoever built this place wanted something hidden."

The water rippled.

Nagu drew back.

A voice emerged from the darkness below.

"You have finally arrived."

Nagu's hood rose.

"Who speaks?"

"Someone who remembers the forest before your kind learned to fear the lion."

Nagu stared into the water.

Two enormous eyes opened beneath the surface.

They were so large that Nagu could not see where they ended. Their colour was impossible to identify in the darkness.

He tried to move.

He could not.

"What are you?" he whispered.

The creature did not answer.

Instead, it showed him a vision.

An ancient kingdom.

A mighty lion king.

A serpent emblem hidden beneath a throne.

And a stone seal that had been carried away when the old kingdom fell.

Nagu's breathing quickened.

"The Serpent Seal."

The eyes remained fixed on him.

"Find it," the voice commanded. "And the forest will remember who ruled it before the lions."

Nagu swallowed.

"Why should I trust you?"

A low sound moved through the water. It might have been laughter.

"You should not."

The eyes vanished.

The pool became still.

Nagu waited until dawn, but the creature did not return.

He should have fled.

Instead, he began searching.

For months, he followed the strange symbols through abandoned tunnels, broken temples, and forgotten territories. Every clue brought him closer to the seal. Every discovery showed him that the ancient serpent rulers had once held power over the entire forest.

At last, beneath the ruins of a collapsed sanctuary, Nagu found a small chamber sealed by stone.

Inside lay an object shaped like a coiled serpent, its eyes carved from dark green crystal.

The Serpent Seal.

Nagu lifted it.

The ground trembled.

Somewhere far beneath the earth, something enormous moved.

He clutched the seal against his chest.

For the first time in his life, Nagu smiled without hiding his fangs.

"The lions believe they own this forest," he whispered.

He turned toward the distant Green Crown.

"They have no idea what is coming."

But one question remained unanswered.

How had the creature beneath the water known where the seal was hidden?

And why had it waited so long for Nagu to find it?

While Nagu planned his rise to power, another creature was making plans of his own.

Chinna had never believed in loyalty.

Loyalty was what animals called a promise when they lacked the courage to ask what it was worth.

He preferred bargains.

A handful of food for a secret.

A safe passage for a lie.

A kingdom, if the opportunity ever presented itself.

When Nagu first approached him, the fox was living near the boundary of the Green Crown, stealing eggs from abandoned nests and watching the patrols.

The serpent offered him a place in a greater plan.

"You are clever," Nagu said. "But you waste your gifts on scraps."

Chinna flicked his tail.

"And you offer me what?"

"A chance to stand beside the creature who will rule the forest."

"Beside him?" Chinna asked. "Or beneath him?"

Nagu's eyes narrowed.

"You will have a territory of your own."

Chinna pretended to consider the offer.

Inside, he was already planning how to betray the serpent if a better opportunity appeared.

"Then we have a bargain."

His first assignment was simple.

Whenever a guard grew suspicious, Chinna appeared with another useful piece of information.

Whenever Simha's young allies began to trust one another, Chinna found a reason for them to argue.

But the fox made one mistake.

He underestimated the little lion.

One afternoon, Simha discovered Chinna stealing food from a nest of frightened squirrels.

The fox froze.

The squirrels hid behind a root.

Simha stepped forward.

"Why are you taking their food?"

"Because I am hungry."

"So are they."

Chinna gave a dismissive shrug.

"The forest belongs to whoever can take what they need."

Simha looked at the frightened squirrels.

Then he pushed his own share of berries toward them.

"Not in my forest."

Chinna stared at him.

"You are too soft to rule."

"Perhaps."

"Someone will betray you."

"Perhaps."

"Someone will kill you."

Simha met his eyes.

"Then I hope I have done enough good before that day."

For a moment, Chinna had no reply.

It was the first time anyone had answered his cruelty without anger or fear.

He hated the feeling it left behind.

So he turned away and carried the squirrels' stolen food back to Nagu.

But he did not tell the serpent about Simha's words.

Somewhere deep inside the fox, a tiny doubt had begun to grow.

And Nagu, who trusted nobody, would eventually discover it.

Years passed.

Simha grew into a strong and kind young lion. Bujji remained beside him, still stealing food whenever the king forgot to eat. Puli and Jaga became the most feared guardians of the forest boundary, though they still argued over almost everything.

Then, one stormy night, the eastern watchtower caught fire.

A group of young animals was trapped inside.

Puli raced toward the flames from the northern path. Jaga approached from the eastern ridge.

Both reached the burning tower at the same moment.

"Move!" Puli roared.

"You move!" Jaga snapped.

The tower groaned.

A beam crashed across the doorway.

Inside, the young animals screamed.

Puli charged the beam, but the burning wood collapsed, forcing him back.

Jaga jumped onto the side wall and searched for another entrance.

"There is a gap beneath the roof!" he shouted.

"Then get them out!"

"The ledge is too narrow for you!"

Puli's eyes flashed.

"I can climb it."

"And fall on top of everyone inside?"

Another cry came from the tower.

Puli stopped arguing.

Jaga lowered himself through the gap, disappearing into the smoke.

Moments later, he emerged carrying a frightened rabbit. He went back again and again, rescuing the trapped animals one by one.

But on his final trip, the roof collapsed.

Jaga disappeared beneath the flames.

Puli froze.

Then he charged into the burning tower.

He found the jaguar pinned beneath a fallen beam, struggling to free himself.

"Leave me!" Jaga growled.

Puli braced his shoulder against the wood.

"Be quiet."

"You cannot lift it alone!"

"Then stop talking and push!"

Together, they shifted the beam enough for Jaga to escape.

Puli dragged him clear just as the tower collapsed.

For a long moment, the two warriors lay in the wet grass, breathing hard.

Jaga looked at the tiger.

"You came back."

Puli looked away.

"I needed someone to argue with tomorrow."

Jaga laughed despite his injuries.

It was the first time they had laughed together.

When Simha arrived, he found the two warriors sitting beside one another while Bujji inspected their burns and complained that neither of them had thought to rescue the fruit basket.

Simha looked at Puli and Jaga.

"You protected the forest today."

Puli nodded.

Jaga lowered his head.

"And you did it together," Simha added.

The tiger and jaguar exchanged a glance.

Neither called the other a friend.

Not yet.

But from that day onward, they stopped fighting over every patrol route.

They began training together.

They began trusting one another in battle.

And slowly, the forest's two fiercest rivals became the beginning of something neither had expected.

A friendship.

But the peace did not last.

The Green Crown had enjoyed several peaceful seasons, and the animals had begun to believe the worst days were behind them.

One evening, a flock of frightened birds descended upon the royal clearing.

"Predators!" they cried. "A cruel pack has entered the southern forest!"

Simha immediately called Puli and Jaga.

"Take the border guards. Protect the smaller animals first. Do not allow the predators to reach the river."

Bujji jumped onto a nearby branch.

"And what about you?"

Simha looked toward the southern trees.

"I am going to find out why they have returned."

Bujji's smile disappeared.

"Then I am coming with you."

Before Simha could argue, Puli stepped forward.

"So am I."

Jaga appeared beside him.

"Someone must make sure the tiger doesn't get lost."

Puli growled.

"Say that again."

"After the battle."

Simha shook his head, almost smiling.

"Both of you, come with me. But remember, our purpose is to protect the forest, not to prove who is stronger."

Together, they entered the southern woods.

The forest grew darker with every step. Broken branches lay across the trail. The earth was marked with enormous clawprints, and the smell of blood drifted through the trees.

Then they heard laughter.

A terrible, familiar laugh.

From behind a fallen tree emerged a massive, scarred hyena with pale eyes and a torn ear. Several vicious predators followed him.

The leader stared at Simha.

Then he began to laugh even louder.

"Well, well. Look what has grown out of that little cub."

Simha stepped forward.

"Who are you?"

The hyena tilted his head.

"You don't remember me?"

Simha's claws pressed into the earth.

The predator smiled.

"I remember you, little prince. I remember your father's face when he saw us surrounding his family. I remember the way he fought. I remember how he almost killed me."

Simha stopped breathing for a moment.

The valley.

The blood.

His father's final smile.

"You were there," he whispered.

"Oh, I was more than there," the hyena replied. "I was the one who helped bring your great king down."

Bujji moved closer to Simha.

"Simha, don't listen to him."

But the hyena was not finished.

"Do you remember the little lion your father tried to save? The cub who looked just like you?"

Simha's eyes widened.

"That cub wasn't you," the predator continued. "We had caught another young lion. Your father saw him in our claws and thought it was his own son. For one precious moment, the mighty Surya forgot the battle."

The hyena stepped forward.

"That moment cost him his life."

Simha trembled with rage.

"You killed my father."

"Yes."

The hyena's smile widened.

"And your mother? Ah, your mother fought beautifully. Even after your father fell, she refused to abandon you. She dragged you toward the rocks while we chased her."

Simha's breathing became heavy.

"We caught her later," the predator continued. "She was injured, exhausted, and still trying to find her cub. She begged us to tell her where you were."

The hyena's voice became crueler.

"We told her nothing. We killed her and left her beneath the rocks. We thought the royal bloodline had ended that night."

For a moment, the entire forest seemed to disappear.

Simha could hear only his own heartbeat.

His mother.

The last creature who had held him when he cried.

The mother who had hidden him, fed him, and tried to protect him after his father's death.

Gone.

Not lost in the wilderness.

Not taken by illness.

Murdered by the creature standing before him.

The hyena leaned closer.

"And now look at you. A king who thinks kindness makes him strong."

Simha lowered his head.

Bujji had never seen his friend like this.

The lion's whole body shook. His claws carved deep lines into the earth. His eyes, usually warm and gentle, burned with a fury that frightened even his allies.

"Simha," Bujji whispered.

The king did not answer.

The hyena laughed.

"Come on, little prince. Show me what your father taught you."

Simha roared.

It was not the roar of a gentle king.

It was the roar of a son who had just learned that the deepest wounds of his life had been caused on purpose.

He charged.

The hyena barely had time to raise his claws before Simha struck him across the face. The predator crashed against a tree.

The remaining attackers rushed forward.

Puli intercepted the first, driving it away from the wounded animals hiding behind the rocks. Jaga vanished into the undergrowth and struck the enemies from their blind side. Bujji climbed a twisted tree, swung down on a branch, and knocked a predator away from Simha's flank.

"Simha!" Bujji shouted. "Don't let your anger make you forget who you are!"

But the king could barely hear him.

He fought like his father.

Every movement was powerful. Every strike carried the weight of years of grief. The predators who had terrorised the southern forest found themselves facing a king who refused to retreat.

The hyena recovered and attacked from behind.

Simha turned at the last moment.

Their bodies collided, shaking the ground beneath them.

"You should have died with your parents!" the hyena snarled.

Simha drove him backward.

"No," he growled. "I survived so that creatures like you would never do this to another family."

The hyena lunged.

Simha met him head-on.

The predator struck Simha's shoulder, and pain shot through the lion's body. Simha stumbled. The hyena saw his chance and rushed forward, aiming for his throat.

"SIMHA!" Bujji screamed.

Simha twisted aside. The attack missed by a hair, but the hyena's claws tore through his fur.

For a moment, the lion could not move.

He could feel the blood running down his leg.

The hyena stepped closer.

"Look at you. You are bleeding, just like your father."

Simha's eyes burned.

The hyena lowered his head.

"Do you want to know what your mother said before she died?"

Simha froze.

Bujji saw the change in his friend's face.

"Don't listen!" the monkey shouted.

But the hyena continued.

"She called your name. Again and again. Even when she could no longer stand, she called for her little cub."

Simha's breathing became uneven.

The world around him seemed to slow.

He could almost hear his mother's voice.

He could almost feel her holding him beneath the ruined treehouse.

Then the hyena laughed.

"And you weren't there to save her."

Something inside Simha broke.

He charged with a roar that shook the trees.

The hyena tried to stop him, but Simha drove him backward with every ounce of strength he had left. They crashed through the bushes, tearing branches from the trees. The predator struck again and again, but Simha refused to fall.

He was no longer thinking about the crown.

He was not thinking about the kingdom.

He was thinking about a father who had smiled before dying.

A mother who had called his name.

A little cub who had waited for someone who would never return.

"YOU TOOK EVERYTHING FROM ME!" Simha roared.

The hyena stumbled.

Simha struck him down.

For one moment, the predator lay still.

Then he tried to rise again.

Simha stood over him, his chest heaving, his eyes full of pain.

The hyena spat at his paws.

"Finish it, little king. Become the monster you were born to fight."

Simha raised his paw.

Bujji watched in horror.

He knew his friend could kill the predator.

He also knew that if Simha let his anger rule him completely, the memory of this battle might haunt him for the rest of his life.

"SIMHA!" Bujji shouted. "LOOK AT ME!"

The lion did not turn.

"Simha, please!"

The hyena laughed weakly.

"Listen to your little friend. Perhaps he can teach you how to cry."

Simha's paw trembled above the predator.

His father's face appeared in his mind.

Then his mother's.

Then the faces of the frightened animals waiting behind the rocks.

He remembered the promise he had made to himself.

He would never allow another family to suffer the way his had suffered.

But he could not allow the hyena to attack another family either.

The predator suddenly sprang upward, aiming straight for Simha's throat.

Simha moved before he could think.

He turned, struck the attacker with all his remaining strength, and brought the fight to an end.

The hyena fell and did not rise again.

For a long moment, Simha stood motionless.

The other predators tried to flee.

But Puli blocked the path, Jaga cut off their escape through the trees, and Bujji guided the frightened animals away from the fighting.

Simha faced the surviving attackers.

"Your reign of terror ends here."

The battle continued until the last of the attacking predators had been defeated.

The forest fell silent, broken only by the sounds of frightened animals emerging from their hiding places.

Simha stood in the centre of the clearing, breathing heavily.

He had avenged his parents.

Yet he did not feel victorious.

He felt empty.

Bujji approached slowly.

"Simha?"

The lion looked at his paws.

"I killed them all."

Bujji swallowed.

"They were going to kill more innocent animals."

"I know."

Simha's voice cracked.

"But I wanted them to suffer. I wanted them to feel the pain I felt."

He looked toward the dark trees.

"For a moment, I wasn't fighting to protect the forest. I was fighting because I hated them."

Bujji placed a hand on his shoulder.

"You were hurt, Simha. You were angry. That doesn't erase the good you have done."

Simha closed his eyes.

"What if I become like them?"

"You won't," Bujji replied. "Do you know why?"

The lion shook his head.

"Because you are frightened of becoming cruel. They were proud of it."

Puli approached, his face marked with scratches.

"You saved the animals today," the tiger said. "You gave them a chance to live."

Jaga nodded.

"And when the battle was over, you stopped. You did not turn on us. You did not attack the helpless."

Simha looked at them.

For years, Puli and Jaga had fought each other over every disagreement. Yet that day, they had trusted one another without hesitation.

They had protected his back while he confronted the enemy who had destroyed his family.

Bujji smiled faintly.

"You don't have to carry everything alone, old friend."

Simha lowered his head.

Then, for the first time since learning the truth about his mother, he allowed himself to cry.

Bujji held him.

Puli stood guard.

Jaga watched the forest.

And the three animals who had once struggled to trust one another stayed beside their king until the first light of morning.

From that day forward, their bond became stronger than rivalry.

Puli and Jaga still argued. They still challenged each other. But when danger threatened one of them, the other was always there.

They had discovered what Simha had been trying to teach them all along.

Strength was not the ability to defeat your enemy. It was the courage to stand beside your friends when the world was falling apart.

And Simha learned something too.

His father's death had taught him the pain of losing a family.

The hyena's cruelty had shown him how easily grief could become hatred.

But Bujji, Puli, and Jaga had reminded him why he had to remain the kind of king his father would have been proud of.

He would never forget his parents.

He would never forgive cruelty.

But he would not allow hatred to rule his kingdom.

By the time Simha became king, the Green Crown had changed.

The young lion who once depended on Bujji for stolen fruit now settled disputes between rival herds, protected the weakest animals, and listened even when his advisers disagreed with him.

Puli became his chief border commander.

Jaga became the guardian of the eastern forest.

Rudra's travelling wolf pack was welcomed whenever it passed through the kingdom.

Bujji remained the king's closest friend—and the only creature permitted to interrupt royal meetings by throwing fruit at the throne.

But Nagu watched the kingdom grow stronger.

He hated the way animals trusted Simha.

He hated the way Puli and Jaga had learned to fight side by side.

Most of all, he hated the fact that Simha's kindness created loyalty that no serpent's threat could command.

One evening, Nagu sent a messenger to the Green Crown with a demand.

The serpent wanted the northern river, the eastern caves, and free access to the forest's underground tunnels.

If Simha refused, Nagu threatened to poison the waterholes.

The royal council demanded war.

Puli wanted to strike first.

Jaga proposed a night attack.

Bujji suggested stealing Nagu's food and waiting for him to become too hungry to fight.

Simha listened to them all.

Then he asked a question.

"How many innocent animals will die if we attack the marsh?"

Nobody answered.

"How many will die if we allow Nagu to poison the river?"

Again, silence.

Simha stood.

"We will protect the river. We will guard the borders. But we will not become cruel simply because our enemy is cruel."

Puli bowed.

Jaga bowed.

Bujji sighed.

"Fine. But if the snake tries to eat me, I am becoming cruel immediately."

Simha laughed.

That night, the king walked alone beneath the apple tree.

He looked up at the stars and remembered his father.

He remembered the valley, the battle, and the smile Surya had given him before he died.

"I understand now," Simha whispered.

The wind moved through the leaves.

He did not know that Nagu had already begun preparing the next betrayal.

He did not know that the Serpent Seal could awaken something hidden beneath the oldest waters of the forest.

And he did not know that the mysterious creature which had guided Nagu was waiting for a moment when the Green Crown would become vulnerable again.

But he knew one thing.

His father had died protecting him.

And he would spend his life protecting others.

The next warning came from the Mongoose Kingdom.

One morning, Commander Puli discovered frightened mongooses arriving at the southern boundary. Their fur was dusty, their paws were bleeding, and several carried wounded members of their family.

Jaga stepped forward. "What happened to you?"

The mongoose king, Mangal, emerged from the group. Though exhausted, he held his head high.

"A cruel band of predators attacked our kingdom," Mangal said. "They came for our territory and slaughtered those who stood in their way. We fought to protect our families, but they were too many."

Before Puli could answer, mocking laughter echoed from the trees.

The predators appeared along the ridge, dragging a wounded mongoose warrior into the open. Their leader threw the small warrior to the ground and laughed at the terrified kingdom.

"Look at them!" the predator chief jeered. "The mighty mongooses! They squeak and scratch, and still they cannot protect their own!"

The mongoose warrior tried to rise, but a predator struck him down. The others laughed again. Mangal charged forward, desperate to save his warrior, but the predator chief knocked him aside.

Simha heard the cries and rushed into the clearing with Puli, Jaga, and Bujji.

"Step away from them!" Simha roared.

The predators turned and burst into cruel laughter.

"A lion has come to save the little creatures!" their leader mocked. "Will you cry for them when they fall?"

Simha did not answer. He charged straight into the predators, forcing them away from the wounded mongooses. Puli attacked from one side while Jaga struck from the shadows. Bujji climbed through the branches, shouting warnings and guiding the smaller animals to safety.

The predator chief rushed at Simha, but the young lion stood firm. He dodged the attack and drove the chief backward, giving Mangal time to gather his surviving warriors.

Then a terrible cry rose from the ground. The mongoose warrior the predators had mocked lay motionless. The enemy had killed him while laughing at his helplessness.

Mangal stared at his fallen warrior. Grief filled his eyes, but he did not retreat. He rallied the remaining mongooses and stood beside Simha.

Together, they pushed the predators back. When the enemy chief finally fled into the southern woods, his laughter faded among the trees.

Simha helped the wounded mongooses reach safety. He stayed with them until the injured had been treated and the families were protected.

Mangal approached the young lion, his voice heavy with grief and gratitude.

"You came when our kingdom was falling. You risked your life for creatures who could offer you nothing in return."

Simha lowered his head respectfully.

"No kingdom should have to face cruelty alone. The strength of a forest is measured by how it protects the weakest among us."

Mangal looked at Simha with solemn respect.

"I am Mangal, king of the mongooses. I give you my word: one day, when danger finds you and you need help, my kingdom will return this favour. We will remember the lion who stood beside us when others laughed at our suffering."

Simha nodded, not knowing how important that promise would become.

Far above them, hidden among the leaves, Chinna the fox had watched everything. He had seen Simha win the mongooses' loyalty through courage rather than fear.

And Chinna understood that this bond might one day stand in the way of Nagu's plans.

That night, Mangal and his surviving people were welcomed under Simha's protection. But Nagu's spies carried news into the Venom Marsh: the lion king had helped the Mongoose Kingdom, and King Mangal had sworn to repay him.

Nagu slowly uncoiled from his black stone throne.

"Then we must make sure the mongoose king never gets the chance," he whispered.

His eyes gleamed as he turned toward the old map of the animal world. The Serpent Seal lay at its centre, beside five words carved into the ancient stone:

Only one king may remain.

Nagu smiled.

"Let the predators weaken the lion. When Simha finally needs his allies, we will see whether his promises can save him."

The final battle began.

Puli attacked from the front. Jaga struck from the shadows. Mangal and the mongoose warriors surrounded the Black Lion's soldiers, while Bujji used the forest's hidden paths to lead the smaller animals to safety.

Chinna, the fox, made his choice.

He raced into Nagu's tunnels and released the captured animals the serpent had intended to use as hostages.

When Nagu discovered the betrayal, he lunged at the fox.

"You belong to no side!"

Chinna narrowly escaped his fangs.

"Correct," the fox replied. "I belong to myself."

But as he fled, he stole the Serpent Seal from Nagu's chamber.

Without it, the snake's army lost its command signal. The serpents scattered, and Nagu was forced to retreat into the deepest marsh.

Kaalakesari fought until he could no longer stand.

Surrounded by the united animals, he refused to surrender.

He charged Bujji one last time.

Rudra and Puli intercepted him, while Jaga struck from behind. Together, they forced the Black Lion over the same cliff where Simha had fallen.

Kaalakesari disappeared into the ravine.

The war was over.

But the victory felt like a funeral.

Because the king who had brought them together would never return to see it.

The animals searched the ravine for three days.

They found Simha's broken royal necklace caught between two rocks.

They found traces of his golden fur.

But they never found his body.

Bujji refused to leave the search.

Rudra approached him.

"We must return to the forest."

Bujji did not look up.

"What if he is alive?"

Rudra had no answer.

At sunset, the animals gathered beneath the banyan tree where Simha had once listened to the smallest creatures.

No one wanted the throne.

Puli believed the kingdom needed a strong warrior. Jaga believed it needed strict laws. Rudra refused to rule a territory that was not his own.

And Bujji wanted nothing but his friend back.

Finally, the monkey climbed onto the empty throne.

"I am not a king," he said. "I cannot roar like Simha. I cannot fight like Puli or Jaga. I cannot lead a pack like Rudra."

He looked around at the grieving animals.

"But I knew the king when he had nothing. I knew him before the crown, before the battles, before the stories people will tell about him."

His voice broke.

"He never asked whether an animal deserved his kindness. He simply gave it."

Bujji placed Simha's necklace upon the throne.

"So until we find a ruler worthy of his legacy, this throne will remain empty."

The animals bowed their heads.

For the first time in generations, the Green Crown had no king.

But it had a promise.

And far away, beneath the poisoned waters of the Venom Marsh, Nagu opened his eyes.

The serpent had lost the battle.

He had not lost the war.

In his coils lay a fragment of the Serpent Seal, glowing with an unfamiliar red light.

A voice emerged from the darkness.

"You failed to kill the lion's kingdom."

Nagu lowered his hood.

"Who are you?"

Two enormous eyes opened beneath the water.

"Someone who remembers what happened before the first king was crowned."

Nagu slowly smiled.

The next war would not be fought for the forest.

It would be fought for the truth behind its creation.

Many years later, after the great war that took King Simha's life, the animals gathered once more beneath the ancient apple tree.

Bujji sat beside the empty stone.

Simha's son rested against his shoulder, listening to the story of the father he had lost.

Puli sat near Jaga.

For once, neither warrior argued.

Rudra's son had grown into a strong wolf, and the two old friends watched him with quiet pride. Chinna sat at the edge of the gathering, his face unreadable.

Bujji finished telling the story of Simha's childhood.

"And that," he told the cub, "is why your father became the king we loved."

Simha's son looked toward the stars.

"Do you think he can hear us?"

Bujji smiled sadly.

"I think that if love can reach beyond the sky, then your father has heard every word."

The night grew quieter beneath the apple tree.

The little lion looked up.

"Can I speak to my father?"

Bujji's heart ached.

"You can speak to him whenever you want."

The cub stood and walked a few steps away from the gathering.

Above the apple tree, the stars shone brightly.

He searched the sky until he found a cluster of stars that seemed to form the outline of a great lion. A broad mane. A powerful head. Eyes that appeared to watch over the forest.

The cub's breath caught.

"Father?"

A gentle breeze passed through the branches.

The stars shimmered.

For one extraordinary moment, the shape of a golden lion seemed to stand above the forest, looking down at his son.

The little lion's eyes filled with tears.

"I miss you."

Nobody spoke.

"I tried to eat today," he continued, his small voice trembling. "Bujji uncle told me stories about you. He told me you were brave. He told me you protected everyone."

He looked at the empty stone.

"I want to be like you, Father. But I'm afraid."

A tear rolled down his cheek.

"What if I cannot protect them? What if I am not strong enough to become a good king?"

Bujji covered his mouth with one hand.

Puli lowered his head.

Jaga's eyes shone in the moonlight.

The little lion looked back toward the sky.

Then he straightened his shoulders.

"You taught everyone to be kind. You taught them to stand together, even when they were enemies."

His voice grew stronger.

"I will do the same."

He lifted his tiny face toward the stars.

"Father, do good and take care of all of them."

He paused, realising what he had said, and smiled through his tears.

"No. I will do good, Father. And I will take care of all of them, just as you did."

The breeze moved through the apple tree again.

A single white flower drifted down and landed on the old stone.

The cub gently picked it up.

"Goodnight, Father."

Above him, the stars seemed to shine a little brighter.

Bujji walked over and placed a hand on the cub's shoulder.

"He heard you," the monkey whispered.

"How do you know?"

Bujji looked up at the sky.

"Because your father always listened when someone needed him."

The little lion leaned against Bujji.

For the first time since Simha's death, he smiled without forcing himself to.

Around them, the animals began sharing their own memories of Simha.

They remembered the battles he had stopped, the families he had saved, and the enemies he had offered a chance to change.

They remembered how he had protected Bujji when others laughed at the little monkey. They remembered how he had brought Puli and Jaga together, how he had welcomed Rudra's pack, and how he had given even the smallest creature a place in his kingdom.

For a little while, the forest felt alive again.

But far beyond the Green Crown, beneath a forgotten lake, the water began to move.

Two enormous eyes opened in the darkness.

The creature had listened to every story.

It had heard the animals praise the lion who had defeated Kaalakesari. It had heard them speak of the Serpent Seal, the war, and the king's final sacrifice.

And it had heard something else.

They were no longer afraid.

They had turned Simha's death into a legend.

The creature slowly rose beneath the surface.

A shadow larger than any serpent moved through the ancient water.

"Still telling stories about the lion," it murmured.

The lake trembled.

"Still believing that his kingdom belongs to them."

The enormous eyes narrowed.

"Let them remember his kindness."

A deep, terrible sound rolled beneath the water.

"Let them celebrate his sacrifice."

The creature moved toward a hidden passage leading to the heart of the forest.

"Because it is time."

The darkness swallowed its enormous shape.

And as the animals continued listening to stories of Simha beneath the apple tree, an ancient voice echoed through the buried tunnels.

"I am coming out of the shadows."

A pause.

"And I will rule this kingdom as my great father ruled it before the lions stole it from us."

The water went still.

"Simha's great father took what belonged to my bloodline."

The creature's eyes glowed beneath the surface.

"And now, his son's kingdom will pay the debt."

Above the lake, the first storm cloud covered the stars.

The forest did not notice.

Not yet.
