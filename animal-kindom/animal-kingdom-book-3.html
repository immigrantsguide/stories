An Original Animal Fantasy

THE ANIMAL KINGDOM

Book Three: The Fall of the Serpent

When one enemy falls, another rises. When a kingdom thinks it is safe, the deadliest danger may already be beneath its feet.

CREATED BY VIJAY KRISHNA

The Green Crown was slowly learning to live without King Simha.

His son was growing, but he was still young. He had his father's golden eyes and gentle heart, yet he sometimes wondered whether kindness alone could protect a kingdom.

Every morning, Rudra taught him the duties of a leader.

"A king must listen before he gives orders," the old wolf explained as they walked beside the river. "He must know when his animals are afraid, when they are hungry, and when they are hiding the truth."

The young lion looked at him.

"What if they make mistakes?"

"Then you help them learn."

"What if they betray me?"

Rudra stopped walking.

"Then you protect your kingdom without allowing their betrayal to turn you into a cruel king."

The little lion thought about his father.

He wanted to become brave like Simha. He wanted to protect the forest as Simha had done. But sometimes, when the night became quiet, he still wished his father would return and tell him what to do.

Rudra seemed to understand.

"You do not have to become your father in one day," he said. "You only have to learn from him every day."

Training was not always peaceful.

Puli taught the young lion how to stand his ground, control his breathing, and defend himself against a stronger opponent. Jaga taught him how to move silently, study an enemy's weaknesses, and escape a trap before it closed.

Puli was strict.

"Again!" the tiger roared.

The little lion tried to push against a heavy log, but it rolled back and knocked him into the grass.

Jaga shook his head.

"You are fighting the log instead of thinking about how to move it."

Puli growled.

"He needs strength."

"And he needs a brain," Jaga replied.

"Are you saying I have no brain?"

"I'm saying the log has defeated you both."

From a nearby branch, Bujji burst into laughter.

The young lion laughed too.

For a moment, the training ground felt like the old days, when Simha and Bujji had spent entire afternoons arguing over stolen fruit.

The young lion was not alone anymore.

Rudra's son became his closest companion. The two raced through the forest, climbed fallen trees, and splashed in shallow streams. Puli's daughter often joined them, and she was faster than both of them.

Whenever the young lion lost a race, she reminded him that being the king's son did not make him the fastest animal in the forest.

Whenever Rudra's son lost, he blamed the wind.

And whenever Bujji caught them arguing, he told them one of his endless stories about how he had once defeated five guards, stolen an entire basket of mangoes, and escaped without leaving a single footprint.

Nobody believed the story.

But everybody laughed.

The animals were beginning to smile again.

And far away, beneath the dark waters of the Venom Marsh, Nagu was preparing to destroy that happiness.

One afternoon, as the young lion returned from training, Chinna appeared beside the old apple tree.

The fox looked thinner than before. His reddish fur was covered in dust, and one of his ears had a fresh cut.

Bujji narrowed his eyes.

"What have you stolen now?"

Chinna smiled.

"Only something that could save your entire forest."

"Last time you said that, three deer spent the night hiding inside my fruit store."

"They were very comfortable deer."

"Chinna!"

The fox's smile disappeared.

"I am serious, Bujji. Nagu is preparing something terrible."

The young lion stepped forward.

"What is he planning?"

Chinna looked at the surrounding animals before answering.

"He is going to poison the river."

Nobody spoke.

The river supplied water to the Green Crown, the wolf territories, and several smaller animal settlements. If it became poisonous, hundreds of animals could die before they understood what had happened.

"How do you know?" Rudra asked.

"Because I have been watching Nagu."

Bujji folded his arms.

"And why should we trust you?"

Chinna looked down.

"You shouldn't trust me because I ask you to. Trust the information. Nagu has gathered his strongest snakes near the old stone bridge. His servants have carried poison into the underground channels. Tonight, they will release it into the water."

Rudra immediately turned toward the northern trail.

"My pack is preparing to leave tomorrow. We must warn the animals along the river before we go."

"Tomorrow will be too late," Chinna replied.

Rudra looked at him.

"Then we leave now."

The wolf leader called his warriors. Puli and Jaga prepared the border guards, while Bujji took the young lion somewhere safe.

But the little lion refused to leave.

"My father would not hide while other animals were in danger."

Bujji knelt beside him.

"Your father also knew when to protect the future of his kingdom."

The young lion looked toward the river.

"I am not asking to lead the battle. I am asking to help save the animals."

Rudra watched him carefully.

Then the old wolf nodded.

"You will stay behind the main fighters. You will listen to your commanders, and you will never leave your companions to chase an enemy."

The young lion agreed.

Bujji touched his forehead.

"Your father would be proud of you."

Together, they hurried toward the river.

The sun was disappearing when they reached the old stone bridge.

At first, everything appeared peaceful.

The water flowed between the rocks. Birds flew over the trees. A few deer drank near the bank.

Then Rudra noticed something strange.

The birds were not landing near the water.

The deer were refusing to drink.

And beneath the bridge, hundreds of small movements disturbed the muddy ground.

Rudra raised his paw.

"Everyone, stop."

A thin hiss came from the darkness.

Then another.

Then hundreds.

The snakes had arrived.

Nagu's army rose from the water, slid between the roots, and emerged from holes beneath the bridge. Some were small and quick. Others were thick, powerful serpents with long fangs and deadly venom.

Their bodies covered the stones.

Their eyes watched every movement.

At the centre stood Nagu.

His dark scales shone beneath the dying light, and the Serpent Seal hung from a cord around his neck.

He looked at the wolves and laughed.

"Rudra. I thought you would be halfway to the Windlands by now."

Rudra stepped forward.

"I was."

Nagu tilted his head.

"Then why have you returned?"

Rudra's eyes hardened.

"Because your poison will kill innocent animals."

Nagu's tongue flickered.

"That is the purpose."

A low growl passed through the wolf pack.

The young lion felt his heart beating faster.

He had trained with Puli and Jaga. He had listened to Rudra's lessons. He had heard Bujji's stories about battles.

But he had never seen an enemy army like this.

The snakes did not stand in neat rows. They moved around one another, slipped beneath the stones, and disappeared into the water whenever a wolf tried to approach.

They seemed to be everywhere.

Nagu raised his head.

"Look around you, Rudra. Your wolves are strong, but strength cannot protect you from poison. One bite, one mistake, and even your greatest warrior will fall."

He turned toward the young lion.

"And you must be Simha's little heir."

The cub stiffened.

Nagu smiled.

"Your father is dead. Your kingdom is weak. Your protectors are old. Tell me, little prince, how long do you think you can survive without your father's shadow?"

The young lion's claws dug into the earth.

Bujji stepped in front of him.

"Talk to someone your own size, you overgrown shoelace."

Nagu's eyes narrowed.

"Still making jokes, monkey? I wonder how long you will keep laughing when your friends begin to die."

He lowered his head.

"Release the poison."

The snakes began moving toward the river channels.

Rudra roared.

"WOLVES! HOLD THE BRIDGE!"

The battle began.

The first snakes struck before the wolves could form their line. One serpent lunged at Rudra's throat, but he twisted away and knocked it against a stone.

Another wrapped itself around a wolf's leg.

The wolf cried out as its companions rushed to free him.

More snakes poured from beneath the bridge.

They attacked from the front, the sides, and the water. Whenever the wolves pushed one group back, another appeared behind them.

The riverbank became a battlefield.

Rudra's warriors fought with everything they had. They bit, clawed, and pushed the snakes away from the water. But the serpents had chosen the ground carefully.

The muddy banks slowed the wolves.

The narrow bridge made it difficult for them to move together.

And the snakes knew every hidden tunnel beneath the stones.

A young wolf tried to cross the bridge to reach the poison containers.

A serpent struck from a crack in the wall.

The wolf fell.

Rudra rushed toward him, but two snakes blocked the path.

"Move!" Rudra roared.

They attacked together.

Rudra fought them off, reached the wounded wolf, and dragged him away from the water.

But the young warrior was barely breathing.

Rudra lowered his head.

"Stay with me."

The wolf opened his eyes.

"Did we stop them?"

Rudra looked toward the river.

"Not yet."

The young wolf tried to stand.

"I can still fight."

"No," Rudra whispered. "You have fought enough."

The wolf closed his eyes.

Rudra stayed beside him for one painful moment.

Then another scream came from the bridge.

The battle had not stopped.

Rudra rose.

His face was wet with tears, but his voice remained strong.

"Protect the living! Do not let the snakes reach the water!"

The wolves charged again.

Many of the pack's oldest warriors had fought beside Rudra since his earliest journeys. They were slower now, their fur grey, their legs tired, but they knew how to stand together.

They formed a wall across the bridge.

The snakes struck again and again.

One elder wolf fell while protecting two young warriors. Another was pulled into the shallow water and surrounded by serpents. A third managed to drive a group away from the poison containers before a snake bit him from behind.

Rudra saw them fall.

His chest tightened.

These were not strangers.

They were the animals who had carried him when he was young, taught him to hunt, and trusted him to lead them across dangerous lands.

Now they were dying because he had returned to protect a forest that was not even his own.

For one terrible moment, he wondered whether he had made the wrong choice.

Then he remembered Simha.

He remembered the lion king who had welcomed his pack when others called them trespassers.

He remembered the promise he had made beneath the apple tree.

He would protect the forest.

Even if it cost him everything.

"STAND TOGETHER!" Rudra roared.

The remaining wolves answered with a powerful howl.

The sound echoed across the river.

And something in the darkness answered them.

A deep growl rose from the mangrove forest beyond the eastern bank.

The snakes stopped moving.

Nagu's head turned sharply.

"No."

A second growl followed.

Then came the sound of hundreds of paws moving through wet soil.

From between the twisted mangrove roots emerged the mongoose warriors.

They were smaller than the wolves, but their movements were fast and controlled. Their brown bodies slipped through the narrow gaps between roots, and their sharp eyes followed every movement of the snakes.

At their front walked an old, powerful mongoose with a pale scar across his muzzle.

He was King Mangal of the Mangrove Kingdom.

Nagu slowly raised his hood.

"Stay away from my army."

Mangal looked at the poison containers beside the river.

"Your army is poisoning our water."

"Your marsh is not part of my kingdom."

Mangal's expression hardened.

"Water does not belong to one king. It belongs to every creature that needs it to live."

Nagu laughed.

"Then you should have protected it better."

He gave a sharp hiss.

The snakes turned toward the new arrivals.

Mangal raised his paw.

"Mongooses! Protect the river!"

The mangrove warriors charged.

The snakes had terrified smaller animals for generations. Their venom had driven families from their homes and turned once-green waterholes into places of fear.

But the mongooses had lived beside them for a long time.

They knew how quickly a snake could strike.

They knew how to move away from the fangs.

And they knew that a single mistake could mean death.

The first mongoose slipped beneath an attacking serpent and struck its side. Another jumped onto a fallen log and attacked a snake trying to reach a wolf. A third pulled a wounded elder away from the water before the serpent could strike again.

The fight became faster and more dangerous.

The snakes tried to surround the mongooses, forcing them toward the muddy banks. But the mongooses moved through the roots and narrow tunnels, escaping the larger serpents and attacking from unexpected directions.

For the first time that night, Nagu's army began to retreat.

Then the Serpent Seal around his neck started to glow.

Nagu lifted his head.

The snakes suddenly changed their movements.

They stopped retreating and attacked together.

A group of serpents surrounded the mongooses near the bridge. Another group slipped behind the wolves. Several snakes rushed toward the poison containers while the defenders were distracted.

Nagu had planned for this.

He knew that the mongooses would come if the river was threatened. He had prepared a second route to release the poison, one that passed beneath the mangrove roots.

If he could hold the defenders long enough, the water would become poisonous before anyone could stop him.

Chinna watched from a tree above the battlefield.

His eyes widened.

"He has another tunnel!"

He jumped down and raced toward Mangal.

"The poison is going beneath the eastern roots! You must block that channel!"

Mangal turned.

"How do you know?"

"Because I helped Nagu study the river. I know every path he planned to use."

The mongoose king's expression became cold.

"You helped him?"

Chinna lowered his head.

"Yes. And I have spent too long helping cruel animals because I thought it would benefit me."

He looked toward the fighting.

"Let me make this right."

Mangal studied him for a moment.

Then he nodded.

"Show us the tunnel."

Chinna led a group of mongooses through the tangled roots. They found the hidden channel just as the first containers of poison were being carried toward it.

The snakes guarding the entrance attacked immediately.

Chinna darted between them, drawing their attention while the mongooses moved into position.

One serpent lunged toward the fox.

Chinna barely escaped.

"That was a terrible idea!" he shouted as he jumped behind a root.

A mongoose struck the serpent from the side.

"Then stop standing where it can bite you!"

"Excellent advice. I shall remember it when I am alive!"

Together, they drove the guards away from the channel.

Mangal ordered the entrance blocked with stones and fallen branches. The poison containers were pulled away from the water, and the hidden route was sealed.

Nagu felt the change immediately.

The Serpent Seal stopped glowing.

His army had lost its advantage.

The snakes began to panic.

"Hold your positions!" Nagu hissed. "The river must be poisoned!"

But his commands no longer frightened the mongooses.

Mangal advanced across the muddy bank.

"You have lost, Nagu."

The serpent's eyes burned.

"I have not even begun."

He struck at the mongoose king.

Mangal dodged, but Nagu's fangs caught the edge of his shoulder. The king stumbled, pain spreading through his body.

The mongooses roared and rushed forward.

Nagu pulled away and attacked again.

Mangal barely avoided the second strike.

The snake was fast, and the venom was deadly.

Nagu circled him, waiting for the smallest mistake.

"You should have stayed in your marsh," the serpent whispered. "Now you will die protecting a river that will belong to me."

Mangal's breathing grew heavier.

He knew he could not continue dodging forever.

Then he noticed something.

Nagu kept glancing toward the Serpent Seal around his neck.

The serpent was not simply wearing it.

He was using it to command his army.

Mangal stepped back.

Nagu smiled.

"Afraid?"

"No," Mangal replied. "I am learning."

He suddenly charged.

Nagu struck with his fangs.

Mangal turned aside at the last moment and slammed his shoulder against the serpent's body.

The Serpent Seal tore free.

It fell into the mud.

For one brief moment, the battlefield went silent.

Then Chinna appeared from beneath the bridge.

He snatched the seal and raced toward the mangrove roots.

Nagu's eyes widened.

"CHINNA!"

The fox did not stop.

"You promised me a kingdom!" he shouted over his shoulder. "You forgot to mention that you planned to rule over my bones!"

Nagu abandoned Mangal and chased him.

But Chinna had already reached the old mangrove sanctuary, where the roots formed a natural wall around a shallow stone pool.

Mangal followed, despite his injuries.

"Give me the seal!" he ordered.

Chinna held it out.

"Gladly. I have discovered that stealing from snakes is much less stressful than working for them."

Mangal took the seal.

The dark green crystals began to glow.

Ancient symbols appeared across its surface.

The mongoose king stared at them.

He recognised the markings from the oldest stories of his kingdom.

The seal was not simply a symbol of power.

It was a key.

And the snakes had used it to control their army through ancient commands buried in their bloodline.

Mangal raised the seal above the stone pool.

Then he brought it down against the ancient rock.

A sharp crack echoed through the sanctuary.

The Serpent Seal broke.

Green light burst across the water.

The ground trembled.

Far beneath the forest, hidden tunnels collapsed. The glowing marks on the seal faded. Across the battlefield, the snakes suddenly lost their strange unity.

Some fled into the marsh.

Others stopped, confused by the sudden loss of their leader's commands.

Nagu reached the sanctuary just as the last piece of the seal fell into the water.

For the first time, the serpent looked afraid.

"You fool!" he hissed. "You have no idea what you have destroyed!"

Mangal stood between him and the broken pieces.

"Perhaps. But I know what you were going to do with it."

Nagu raised his hood.

"I will tear this forest apart!"

He launched himself at the mongoose king.

Mangal moved aside, but Nagu changed direction in mid-attack. His fangs came dangerously close to Mangal's throat.

The king fell backward.

Nagu turned and struck again.

Mangal rolled away, but the serpent's tail wrapped around his hind leg and pulled him into the mud.

The mongoose king struggled to free himself.

Nagu leaned closer.

"Your kingdom ends here."

Then he heard a growl behind him.

Rudra stood at the entrance to the sanctuary.

The wolf leader was covered in mud and blood. His breathing was heavy, and his legs trembled from exhaustion.

But he was still standing.

Nagu smiled.

"Ah, Rudra. You have come to watch your king die."

He released Mangal and turned toward the wolf.

Rudra tried to stand his ground, but the battle had taken its toll. Several of his oldest warriors were dead. Others lay wounded near the bridge.

Nagu rushed forward.

Rudra moved to defend himself, but his injured leg failed beneath him.

He fell.

The serpent rose above him, his fangs ready.

"Your pack will remember this night," Nagu whispered. "They will remember how their great leader begged for his life."

Rudra looked up at him.

"I will never beg you."

Nagu's fangs came closer.

"Then die proudly."

He struck.

A powerful shape crashed into him from the side.

Mangal had returned.

The mongoose king threw his entire weight against Nagu, knocking the serpent away from Rudra.

Nagu slammed into the stone wall.

Before he could recover, Mangal attacked again.

The serpent fought with every ounce of strength he possessed. He struck with his fangs, wrapped his body around the mongoose king, and tried to drag him into the shallow pool.

Mangal refused to release him.

The two enemies crashed through the mud and broken roots.

Nagu managed to turn his head toward Mangal's throat.

His fangs were only a breath away.

Then Mangal struck with all his strength.

Nagu's body went still.

The serpent's head fell into the mud.

For several moments, Mangal remained beside him, breathing heavily.

The snake who had terrorised the forest for years would never poison another river.

Rudra struggled to his feet.

He looked at Nagu's body, then at the broken Serpent Seal lying in the pool.

"It is over," he whispered.

Mangal shook his head.

"No. The battle is over."

He looked toward the dark forest beyond the sanctuary.

"But danger never disappears simply because one enemy dies."

The surviving snakes fled into the marshes. Some disappeared into underground tunnels. Others scattered across the distant territories, no longer able to hear Nagu's commands.

The mongooses did not chase the wounded animals into hiding. Their king ordered them to secure the river, rescue the injured, and bury the dead.

The wolves searched for their elders.

Rudra found the oldest warrior in his pack lying beneath a fallen branch. The wolf had fought beside him for many years, teaching young pups how to travel safely through unfamiliar lands.

Rudra lowered his head.

"You promised me you would see the Windlands again."

The old wolf's eyes were barely open.

"I have seen enough forests, old friend."

Rudra tried to speak, but his voice broke.

The elder looked toward the river.

"Did we save them?"

Rudra nodded.

"Yes."

The old wolf smiled faintly.

"Then we did not travel here in vain."

His eyes closed.

Rudra remained beside him long after the others had left.

He had led his pack through mountains, rivers, and dangerous territories. He had always believed that a good leader could bring his animals home if he made the right decisions.

That night, he learned that even the best leader could not save everyone.

But he could make sure their sacrifice meant something.

When dawn arrived, Rudra returned to the Green Crown with the surviving wolves.

Mangal travelled beside him.

The mongoose king had been injured in the battle, but he refused to return to his kingdom before making sure the river was safe.

Chinna followed at a distance.

For once, the fox did not ask for a reward.

The young lion waited beneath the apple tree with Bujji, Puli, and Jaga.

When Rudra appeared, the cub ran toward him.

"Where are the others?"

Rudra lowered his head.

The young lion looked behind him.

He saw the wounded wolves.

He saw the empty spaces where the old warriors should have been.

His smile disappeared.

"Where are they, Rudra?"

The wolf leader knelt before him.

"They fought bravely, little king."

The cub's eyes filled with tears.

"Did they die because they came to help us?"

Rudra struggled to answer.

"They died because Nagu chose cruelty over peace. They died protecting the river, the forest, and the animals who could not protect themselves."

The little lion lowered his head.

For a moment, he looked so much like Simha that Bujji had to turn away.

Then the cub stepped forward and placed his small paw on Rudra's shoulder.

"I will remember them."

Rudra looked at him.

"And I will make sure you never forget what they taught us."

Mangal approached the young lion.

"Your father once protected my kingdom without asking what he would receive in return," he said. "Today, we have returned that kindness."

The young lion bowed his head.

"Thank you for saving our forest."

Mangal shook his head.

"We saved the river together. Never forget that, little king. A kingdom survives when its neighbours are willing to stand beside it."

Puli stepped forward.

"Then let us make sure the next enemy finds us ready."

Jaga nodded.

"We will strengthen the borders, guard the rivers, and train the next generation."

Bujji placed a hand on the young lion's head.

"And we will keep telling stories about the animals who made this possible."

The little lion looked around at the survivors.

He had lost his father.

Now he had learned that even brave warriors could die protecting others.

But he had also learned that courage could pass from one generation to the next.

Rudra and Mangal decided to remain in the Green Crown for a time. Rudra would help the young lion learn the duties of leadership, while Mangal's warriors would guard the river and watch for any snakes who tried to return.

Puli and Jaga would continue training the young king, teaching him how to defend himself without allowing anger to control him.

Bujji would remain his friend, his storyteller, and the one animal who could still make him laugh when the world became too heavy.

The forest began to heal.

The river ran clear again.

The mangrove warriors returned to their roots, and the wolves buried their fallen elders beneath the trees they had protected.

For several nights, the animals gathered under the apple tree and shared stories of the battle.

They spoke of the wolves who had stood against the snakes.

They remembered Mangal's courage and Chinna's unexpected help.

And they remembered Nagu, whose hunger for power had destroyed everything he had tried to control.

But Chinna's part in the story was not finished.

One evening, as the fox sat alone near the river, he remembered the broken Serpent Seal.

He had stolen it from Nagu.

He had delivered it to Mangal.

And he had watched it break.

Yet one thought continued to trouble him.

If the seal had been hidden for so many years, why had the mysterious voice beneath the water wanted Nagu to find it?

Chinna looked toward the distant lake.

The water was perfectly still.

He quickly turned away.

For the first time in his life, the fox was afraid of something he could not trick, outrun, or bargain with.

Far beyond the mangrove forest, beneath the ancient lake, two enormous eyes opened.

A shadow moved beneath the water.

Then the surface broke.

A massive crocodile rose from the darkness.

Its ancient scales were covered with scars. Its jaws were powerful enough to crush a fallen tree, and its eyes held a strange intelligence that made the darkness around it seem alive.

Around its neck hung a heavy necklace.

Dark stones were set into the metal, and each stone held a faint light that seemed to move like a sleeping eye.

The crocodile slowly climbed onto the muddy bank.

It looked toward the distant Green Crown.

Then it lowered its head and closed its eyes.

Far away, the young lion was sleeping beneath the apple tree.

In his dream, he saw his father standing on a mountain beneath a red sky.

Simha looked older than the last time the cub had seen him in the stars.

His golden mane was covered in shadows.

Behind him stood hundreds of lions, their eyes glowing in the darkness.

The young lion tried to run toward his father, but the ground beneath his paws began to crack.

A deep voice echoed through the dream.

Your father took what belonged to us.

The mountain trembled.

His bloodline will answer for it.

The young lion woke with a cry.

Bujji rushed to his side.

"What happened?"

The cub struggled to breathe.

"I saw Father."

Bujji sat beside him.

"Was he hurt?"

The young lion shook his head.

"No. He was trying to warn me."

Far away, the crocodile opened its eyes.

The stones in its necklace glowed brighter.

It had seen the cub's dream.

It had felt his fear.

And it had learned something important about the young heir to the Green Crown.

The crocodile smiled.

"Interesting."

Its powerful tail swept through the water.

"Simha's son can see his father among the stars. Perhaps he will be easier to control than the old king."

The crocodile turned toward the distant forest.

The necklace pulsed again.

This time, it showed the crocodile a different vision: the young lion standing beside Rudra, Mangal, Puli, Jaga, and Bujji beneath the apple tree.

The animals were laughing.

They were telling stories of Simha.

They believed the worst was behind them.

The crocodile watched them through the dream.

Then it spoke in a voice older than the oldest trees.

"Let them celebrate the death of the serpent."

Its jaws opened slightly.

"Let them believe their kingdom is safe."

The crocodile slipped back into the water.

"Because the next king they face will not need an army to conquer them."

The necklace glowed in the darkness.

"He will enter their minds before they even know he has arrived."

The water closed over its head.

And somewhere beneath the Green Crown, an ancient passage began to open.

THE END
