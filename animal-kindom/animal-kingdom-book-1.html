An Original Animal Fantasy

THE ANIMAL KINGDOM

Book One: The Last Roar

A king's promise. A forest divided. A final roar that changes every kingdom.

CREATED BY VIJAY KRISHNA

Long before the war began, the animals of the Green Crown believed that no creature could destroy their peace.

King Simha had made one promise to his father before the old king died.

“No animal shall fear another animal merely because it is smaller or weaker.”

Simha kept that promise.

When a rabbit lost its home in a flood, the king ordered his soldiers to find it shelter. When an old elephant could no longer walk, Simha personally visited her. When two rival herds fought over a waterhole, he settled the dispute without drawing a single claw.

Some called him weak.

Others called him the greatest king the forest had ever known.

But his best friend, Bujji, knew the truth.

One evening, while Simha sat beside an ancient banyan tree, Bujji dropped from a branch and landed directly on the king's head.

“Your Majesty, I have terrible news.”

Simha sighed. “What did you steal this time?”

“Nothing.”

“Then the news must truly be terrible.”

Bujji grinned, but his expression quickly changed.

“The northern birds have stopped singing.”

Simha lifted his head.

Far away, beyond the mountains, a black cloud of dust rose above the horizon.

And beneath the roots of the banyan tree, a pair of green eyes slowly opened.

Nagu had awakened.

The war had begun.

At sunrise, Commander Puli discovered pawprints crossing the northern boundary.

There were dozens of them.

Wolf tracks.

He immediately summoned Jaga.

“Foreign animals have crossed our border,” Puli growled.

Jaga examined the ground. “Not hunters. Travellers.”

“Travellers who ignore our laws are still intruders.”

Before Jaga could answer, a grey wolf stepped out from between the trees. Behind him stood a pack of exhausted wolves, including young pups and wounded elders.

The wolf leader bowed his head.

“I am Rudra of the Windlands. We seek passage through your forest.”

Puli's claws dug into the soil.

“No pack crosses the Green Crown without the king's permission.”

“We asked at the river crossing,” Rudra replied. “Your sentries never returned.”

Jaga's eyes narrowed.

“What did you say?”

Rudra looked directly at him.

“We saw their bodies. They were killed by lions.”

The forest fell silent.

Puli sprang forward.

“You dare accuse our kingdom?”

Rudra dodged the tiger's attack. Jaga charged from the side, forcing the wolves to scatter. Within seconds, the boundary became a battlefield.

The wolves fought only to protect their young. Puli fought to defend his kingdom. Jaga fought because he believed every stranger was a possible enemy.

Rudra could have bitten Puli's throat when the tiger slipped on a wet stone.

Instead, he stepped back.

“I did not come here to kill you!”

Puli answered with another attack.

The fight ended only when a wounded wolf pup cried out beneath a fallen branch.

Rudra abandoned his defence and rushed to save it.

Puli froze.

An enemy who risked his life for a pup instead of winning a battle was not the enemy he had expected.

Then Rudra revealed the reason for his journey.

“Kaalakesari has destroyed two territories behind us. His army is coming here. We crossed your boundary because your forest is next.”

Jaga looked at Puli.

For the first time, neither commander knew what to say.

Far above them, hidden among the leaves, Chinna the fox listened to every word.

And smiled.

“Wonderful,” he whispered. “A war before the war.”

He disappeared into the bushes.

He was going to tell Nagu.

But first, he would decide which side would pay him more.

That night, Rudra was brought before King Simha.

Puli stood on the king's right. Jaga stood on his left. Bujji sat on the throne's armrest, eating fruit that clearly did not belong to him.

Simha listened without interruption as Rudra described the fall of the Windlands.

“Kaalakesari does not conquer territories to rule them,” Rudra explained. “He destroys their leaders, takes their strongest warriors, and forces the survivors to serve him.”

Simha's expression darkened.

“How many territories?”

“Three. Ours is the fourth he intends to take.”

“Then you and your pack are no longer trespassers,” Simha said. “You are guests under my protection.”

Puli stepped forward.

“Your Majesty, this is dangerous. If the wolves are lying—”

“Then I will accept responsibility,” Simha replied gently.

Bujji jumped down from the throne.

“And if they are telling the truth, we will need every claw, tooth, and brain in this forest.”

He looked at Puli.

“Yes, Commander. Even yours.”

A reluctant growl escaped the tiger.

For the first time since arriving, Rudra smiled.

But when the council ended, Nagu's spies carried a different message into the Venom Marsh.

The lion king has welcomed the wolves.

Nagu slowly uncoiled from his black stone throne.

“Excellent.”

His servant trembled.

“Excellent, my lord? They may unite against us.”

Nagu's tongue flickered.

“Not if I convince each side that the others are responsible for the coming bloodshed.”

He slid toward a hidden chamber where an old map of the entire animal world lay across the floor.

At its centre was a mark no living king remembered.

The Serpent Seal.

And beside it, written in ancient scratches, were five words:

Only one king may remain.

Nagu smiled.

“Kaalakesari believes he will rule the forest.”

His eyes gleamed.

“Let him win it for me.”

Chinna arrived at Simha's court three days later carrying news about Kaalakesari's army.

He claimed to have escaped from the Ashlands. He described the enemy's numbers, their weapons, and the mountain pass through which they intended to march.

His information was valuable.

Suspiciously valuable.

Bujji pulled Simha aside.

“I don't trust him.”

Simha watched Chinna make the wounded wolves laugh by telling ridiculous stories about Puli's temper.

“Neither do I,” the king admitted.

“Then why let him stay?”

“Because a dishonest fox can still tell the truth when the truth serves him.”

Bujji folded his arms.

“That sounds like something a king says before getting betrayed.”

Simha chuckled.

“Then stay close enough to rescue me.”

That evening, Chinna secretly met Nagu at the river.

“The lion believes you are preparing him for war,” the fox said. “He has started moving his warriors north.”

“And the wolves?”

“They are helping him.”

Nagu's eyes narrowed.

“Good. Now tell Kaalakesari that the lion plans to attack first.”

Chinna hesitated.

“What will you give me when this is over?”

Nagu moved closer.

“A territory of your own.”

The fox looked toward the Green Crown, where golden lights flickered between the trees.

For one brief moment, something like guilt crossed his face.

Then he bowed.

“Agreed.”

But as Chinna left, he took a different path.

He went to the wolves' camp.

And quietly warned Rudra that Nagu was manipulating both kingdoms.

Why?

Because Chinna had discovered something even more frightening than Nagu's ambition.

Nagu had no intention of sharing power with anyone.

Not even Kaalakesari.

The first betrayal came from within the Green Crown.

A storehouse containing the forest's emergency food supply burned to the ground. The guards discovered a piece of wolf fur beside the ashes.

Puli demanded that Rudra and his pack be imprisoned.

The wolves denied everything.

Simha ordered an investigation.

Before dawn, another attack occurred. This time, a young tiger was found wounded near the western river, claiming that wolves had ambushed him.

The forest began turning against its guests.

Even Bujji found himself questioning Rudra.

Then Chinna brought the final piece of evidence: a secret message supposedly written by Rudra, promising Kaalakesari a safe passage into the Green Crown in exchange for protection.

Simha stared at the message.

“Is this yours?” he asked Rudra.

“No.”

“Can you prove it?”

Rudra lowered his eyes.

“I cannot prove what I did not do.”

Simha's silence hurt more than an accusation.

Rudra turned away.

“I thought your kingdom was different.”

The wolves left the court under guard.

That night, Bujji confronted Simha.

“You know something is wrong. Why didn't you defend him?”

“Because a king cannot let friendship replace judgment.”

“And what about our friendship?” Bujji asked. “Will you question that too when someone leaves a piece of my fur beside a crime?”

Simha's ears fell.

“Bujji—”

“No. You can save an entire forest, Your Majesty. Just don't forget the friend who stood beside you before you had a crown.”

Bujji left.

For the first time in their lives, the king and his best friend slept beneath different trees.

And from the shadows, Nagu watched the kingdom begin to fracture.

The truth was revealed by a tiny squirrel.

While searching the burned storehouse, the squirrel had discovered a narrow tunnel beneath the roots. It led to the river, where several snake guards had left their tracks.

The food stores had been poisoned and burned deliberately.

The wolf fur had been stolen from a sleeping pup.

The false message had been written by Chinna under Nagu's orders.

But the squirrel never reached the king.

A serpent intercepted it.

By morning, the little creature was gone.

Nagu's plan was working.

The wolves had been driven away from the court. The tiger and jaguar commanders had begun arguing over who was responsible for the security failures. Simha had lost the confidence of his oldest friend.

And Kaalakesari was marching toward the northern mountains.

Yet Chinna's conscience finally overcame his greed.

He returned to Bujji and confessed everything.

Bujji did not forgive him.

“Why should I believe you now?”

“Because I have something Nagu doesn't know I stole.”

Chinna revealed a small stone tablet bearing the Serpent Seal.

It contained an ancient prophecy about a ruler who would unite the animal kingdoms against a tyrant.

But the last part had been deliberately scratched away.

Bujji stared at it.

“Why would Nagu destroy the ending?”

Chinna swallowed.

“Because I think the prophecy is not about Simha defeating Kaalakesari.”

He looked toward the northern mountains.

“I think it is about what happens after Simha dies.”

Kaalakesari's army reached the northern boundary beneath a sky filled with black clouds.

Thousands of animals fled before them.

At the front marched the Black Lion, his scarred mane whipping in the wind.

“Where is Simha?” he roared.

The answer came from the trees.

“I am here.”

King Simha stood on the ridge with Puli, Jaga, and the forest guards. Bujji stood beside him, carrying a sharpened branch that was far too small for the battle ahead.

Behind them, the wolves emerged from the mist.

Rudra had returned.

He had brought every warrior he could gather from the Windlands.

Simha bowed his head to him.

“I should have trusted you.”

Rudra looked at the approaching army.

“Survive this battle, and we will discuss it.”

The first charge shook the earth.

Puli and the tiger guards struck the enemy's left flank. Jaga led the jaguars through the undergrowth, attacking from behind. Rudra and his wolves formed a moving wall around the smaller animals as they escaped.

Bujji climbed the highest tree and dropped branches onto the advancing soldiers, shouting insults so creative that even the wolves laughed.

For a moment, the Green Crown fought as one kingdom.

Then the snakes attacked from beneath the ground.

Nagu's hidden army erupted from tunnels around the battlefield, striking both sides.

Simha realised the truth.

The snake had never intended to defend the forest.

He had intended to destroy every army capable of resisting him.

“Puli!” Simha roared. “Protect the wounded!”

“Jaga! Seal the eastern tunnels!”

“Rudra! Get the young ones out!”

The commanders obeyed.

But Kaalakesari had reached the ridge.

He faced Simha across the shattered ground.

“Your kingdom ends today,” the Black Lion declared.

Simha lowered his head.

“No. Your cruelty ends today.”

Their collision shook the ridge.

Claws tore through the soil. Their roars echoed across the mountains. Kaalakesari was larger and stronger, while Simha fought with the desperation of a king protecting every life behind him.

The Black Lion drove Simha toward the cliff's edge.

Bujji saw what was happening.

“SIMHA!”

He raced toward them, but a collapsing tree blocked his path.

Rudra abandoned his position to help him.

Puli was trapped beneath a fallen rock. Jaga was fighting three serpents at once. The army was breaking.

And Simha was alone.

Kaalakesari struck Simha across the face.

The king stumbled.

“You could have ruled beside me,” the Black Lion snarled. “Instead, you chose to protect creatures too weak to protect themselves.”

Simha struggled to stand.

“That is exactly why I am their king.”

Kaalakesari attacked again.

Simha fought back, but his strength was fading. Blood darkened his golden fur. Behind him, the cliff overlooked a deep ravine filled with jagged rocks.

Bujji finally broke free.

He raced toward the ridge, screaming his friend's name.

“SIMHA! HOLD ON!”

The king heard him.

For a heartbeat, he saw the little monkey who had once stolen fruit from his father's royal garden, who had shared his childhood, his secrets, his laughter, and his loneliness.

He remembered a promise made beneath the banyan tree.

No animal shall fear another animal merely because it is smaller or weaker.

Kaalakesari lunged toward Bujji.

Simha moved without thinking.

He threw himself between them.

The Black Lion's claws struck the king, and both animals crashed against the edge of the cliff.

Simha caught a narrow root with his front paws.

Kaalakesari stood above him.

The king looked toward Bujji.

“Live,” Simha whispered.

Then he released the root.

Bujji reached the edge just as Simha disappeared into the ravine.

The forest fell silent.

Even the snakes stopped moving.

Bujji stared down into the darkness.

For the first time since childhood, he had no joke to tell.

No plan.

No clever trick.

Only a broken whisper.

“My king…”

He collapsed.

And the greatest roar the Green Crown had ever known was heard no more.

For several moments, nobody moved.

Then Bujji rose.

His tears had not stopped, but something inside him had changed.

He looked at Kaalakesari.

“You killed my brother.”

The Black Lion snarled.

“He was weak.”

Bujji shook his head.

“No. He was the only one among us strong enough to love his enemies without becoming one.”

Rudra stepped forward.

Puli, wounded but standing, joined him. Jaga emerged from the smoke. The surviving forest guards formed a line behind them.

The animals who had once distrusted one another stood shoulder to shoulder.

And for the first time, Kaalakesari saw something he had never understood.

A kingdom did not become powerful because its king was the strongest animal.

It became powerful because its animals chose to stand together.

The final battle began.

Puli attacked from the front. Jaga struck from the shadows. Rudra and his wolves surrounded the Black Lion's soldiers, while Bujji used the forest's hidden paths to lead the smaller animals to safety.

Chinna, the fox, made his choice.

He raced into Nagu's tunnels and released the captured animals the serpent had intended to use as hostages.

When Nagu discovered the betrayal, he lunged at the fox.

“You belong to no side!”

Chinna narrowly escaped his fangs.

“Correct,” the fox replied. “I belong to myself.”

But as he fled, he stole the Serpent Seal from Nagu's chamber.

Without it, the snake's army lost its command signal. The serpents scattered, and Nagu was forced to retreat into the deepest marsh.

Kaalakesari fought until he could no longer stand.

Surrounded by the united animals, he refused to surrender.

He charged Bujji one last time.

Rudra and Puli intercepted him, while Jaga struck from behind. Together, they forced the Black Lion over the same cliff where Simha had fallen.

Kaalakesari disappeared into the ravine.

The war was over.

But the victory felt like a funeral.

Because the king who had brought them together would never return to see it.

The animals searched the ravine for three days.

They found Simha's broken royal necklace caught between two rocks.

They found traces of his golden fur.

But they never found his body.

Bujji refused to leave the search.

On the fourth morning, Rudra approached him.

“We must return to the forest.”

Bujji did not look up.

“What if he is alive?”

Rudra had no answer.

At sunset, the animals gathered beneath the banyan tree where Simha had once listened to the smallest creatures.

No one wanted the throne.

Puli believed the kingdom needed a strong warrior. Jaga believed it needed strict laws. Rudra refused to rule a territory that was not his own.

And Bujji wanted nothing but his friend back.

Finally, the monkey climbed onto the empty throne.

“I am not a king,” he said. “I cannot roar like Simha. I cannot fight like Puli or Jaga. I cannot lead a pack like Rudra.”

He looked around at the grieving animals.

“But I knew the king when he had nothing. I knew him before the crown, before the battles, before the stories people will tell about him.”

His voice broke.

“He never asked whether an animal deserved his kindness. He simply gave it.”

Bujji placed Simha's necklace upon the throne.

“So until we find a ruler worthy of his legacy, this throne will remain empty.”

The animals bowed their heads.

For the first time in generations, the Green Crown had no king.

But it had a promise.

And far away, beneath the poisoned waters of the Venom Marsh, Nagu opened his eyes.

The serpent had lost the battle.

He had not lost the war.

In his coils lay a fragment of the Serpent Seal, glowing with an unfamiliar red light.

A voice emerged from the darkness.

“You failed to kill the lion's kingdom.”

Nagu lowered his hood.

“Who are you?”

Two enormous eyes opened beneath the water.

“Someone who remembers what happened before the first king was crowned.”

Nagu slowly smiled.

The next war would not be fought for the forest.

It would be fought for the truth behind its creation.

And the lion's death was only the beginning.
