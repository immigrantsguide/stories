[ ORIGINAL STORY ]

A timeless love story

Created by Vijay Krishna

It was a beautiful Monday morning in 2020.

The sun had just risen above the busy streets, and the morning breeze carried the fragrance of fresh flowers from the small shops along the road. Meera, a young and beautiful girl with long black hair and sparkling eyes, rode her scooty toward college. Her white dupatta fluttered behind her, and her hair moved gently in the wind.

But her heart was heavy.

She was angry with Vijay.

The previous evening, they had argued over something that seemed small at first but had hurt them both. Vijay had asked her why she had gone out with her friends without informing him. He was worried about her, but Meera felt he was questioning her freedom. Their conversation became an argument, and neither had apologized.

That morning, Vijay was waiting at their usual bus stop.

He had arrived twenty minutes early.

He wore the blue shirt Meera had once told him looked handsome on him. In his hand was a small packet of her favorite chocolates, and inside his bag was a little silver pendant shaped like a heart.

He had spent the previous night thinking about how to make her smile again.

He imagined her stopping her scooty, looking at him angrily for a few seconds, and then laughing when he apologized.

He could already picture her saying, “You are the most irritating boy in the world, Vijay!”

And he would reply, “But you still love me.”

He smiled to himself.

Then he saw her.

“Meera!” he called.

She heard him.

For a brief moment, their eyes met.

Her fingers tightened around the handle of her scooty. She wanted to stop. She wanted to look at him, smile, and tell him that she was sorry too.

But she remembered her father’s words from the previous night.

“From today, you will not speak to that boy. If you continue this relationship, you can forget about studying in this house. I will send you away, and you will never see him again.”

Meera’s eyes filled with tears.

She looked away and continued riding toward college.

Vijay stood there, holding the chocolates.

He watched her disappear around the corner.

For a few seconds, he felt hurt. Then he forced a smile.

“Still angry, huh?” he whispered. “Fine, Meera. I’ll make it up to you today.”

He crossed the road and caught the next bus.

He had no idea that the day would become a memory neither of them would ever forget.

At college, Meera sat beside the classroom window, staring at the trees outside.

Vijay entered and walked straight toward her.

“Good morning.”

She opened her notebook.

He placed a chocolate on her desk.

She moved it aside.

He pulled a chair beside her.

“Meera, are we still fighting?”

She continued writing.

“Yesterday, I shouldn’t have spoken like that. I was worried about you. But I know I should have listened to you too.”

She turned a page.

Vijay looked at her for a moment.

“At least look at me.”

She kept her eyes on the notebook.

He leaned back in his chair.

“Wonderful. I’ve officially become invisible.”

Normally, Meera would have laughed. She would have told him to stop acting like a little boy. She might even have stolen the chocolate and run away before he could catch her.

But that morning, she said nothing.

During the break, Vijay went outside and bought a small bunch of fresh jasmine flowers. He remembered how Meera loved their fragrance. He placed them carefully inside her bag.

Later, he slipped a handwritten note beneath her notebook.

“I’m sorry. I don’t like this silence between us. Can we please talk?”

Meera found the note and read it.

Her eyes filled with tears.

She folded the paper and put it away.

When Vijay returned, he noticed that the flowers were still inside her bag.

He tried again with another chocolate box and a tiny keychain shaped like a heart.

She did not react.

His friends teased him, telling him to stop chasing her.

Vijay smiled along with them, but his heart was hurting.

Every time Meera walked past him without looking at him, he wondered whether he had done something terrible.

He did not know that she was fighting her own battle.

The previous evening, Meera’s father had discovered their relationship.

He was a strict man who believed that parents knew what was best for their children. He had spent his entire life working hard to give his family a respectable place in society. He feared gossip, feared uncertainty, and feared that his daughter’s future would be ruined by a love he did not understand.

“Love does not feed a family!” he had shouted. “Love does not protect a woman when the world turns against her! You are young. You don’t understand life.”

Meera had tried to explain that Vijay was a good person.

Her father refused to listen.

Her mother stood near the kitchen door, tears in her eyes, unable to challenge her husband’s decision.

That night, Meera cried alone in her room.

She wanted to call Vijay and tell him everything.

Instead, she imagined her father discovering the call, confronting Vijay, and making life difficult for both of them.

So she made a decision.

She would stay away from Vijay until she could find a solution.

She thought a few days of silence would protect him.

She did not understand that silence could make someone believe they had lost the person they loved.

After college, Vijay waited near the gate.

He had one final gift in his bag: the little silver pendant.

He wanted to give it to Meera and tell her that no argument was worth losing even a single day of happiness together.

When Meera saw him waiting, she nearly stopped.

For one moment, she wanted to run toward him.

But she remembered her father’s warning.

She started her scooty and left without speaking.

Vijay watched her disappear into the traffic.

This time, he did not smile.

He simply held the pendant inside his pocket and wondered whether tomorrow would be any different.

Meera rode toward her grandmother’s house in the old part of town.

Whenever life became unbearable, she went there.

Her grandmother, Lakshmi, was the only person who could understand her sadness without asking too many questions. The old woman had a way of looking into Meera’s eyes and knowing whether she had laughed, cried, or hidden something from the world.

That evening, Meera wanted to clean the house. Lakshmi had been asking her for several weeks to help arrange the old rooms and sort through the wooden cupboards.

Perhaps keeping herself busy would help her stop thinking about Vijay.

She entered the old house and opened the wooden windows.

The evening breeze moved through the curtains. The house smelled of sandalwood, old books, and the jasmine flowers her grandmother kept in a small brass bowl.

Meera cleaned the shelves, wiped the photographs, and arranged old vessels.

In a room near the courtyard, she discovered a large wooden trunk.

Inside were silk sarees, old family photographs, a silver anklet, and a thick book whose pages had turned yellow with age.

When she opened the book, a blue envelope slipped onto the floor.

She picked it up carefully.

Two words were written on the front.

“For Lakshmi.”

Meera opened it.

Inside was a letter written in beautiful handwriting.

“Dear Lakshmi,

If the first monsoon rain arrives tomorrow, I will wait beneath our banyan tree.

You don’t have to say anything. You never have to promise me anything.

Just come and stand beside me once.

Even if life takes us apart, I will remember this place.

Yours, always,

Ravi.”

The final sentence had been underlined twice.

“Even if life takes us apart, I will remember this place.”

Meera read the letter again.

Something about those words touched a place inside her heart that she could not explain.

She looked toward the window.

Outside stood an enormous banyan tree. Its branches stretched over the old street, and its roots had spread across the ground like the fingers of an ancient hand.

She carried the letter to her grandmother.

“Ammaamma, who is Ravi?”

Lakshmi was holding a cup of tea.

The moment she saw the letter, her hand trembled.

The cup slipped slightly, spilling tea onto the table.

For a long time, she said nothing.

She simply touched the paper with her fingertips, as if she were touching the face of someone she had not seen in decades.

“Where did you find this?” she whispered.

“In the old book. Who is he, Ammaamma?”

Lakshmi looked through the window toward the banyan tree.

A tear rolled slowly down her cheek.

“He was the man I loved before I learned how cruel silence could be.”

Meera sat beside her and held her hand.

“Did you love him very much?”

Lakshmi smiled sadly.

“More than I knew how to say.”

She looked at the letter again.

Then she began telling a story that had waited nearly half a century to be heard.

The year was 1974.

The village was called Vennelapadu, a small, beautiful village near the Godavari River in coastal Andhra Pradesh.

It stood between green paddy fields, coconut gardens, narrow canals, and little waterways that carried the river’s life into the land. The village had no tall buildings, no traffic signals, and no bright advertisements covering every wall.

There were mud roads, tiled-roof houses, wooden doors, and enormous trees that had watched generations of children grow up.

At sunrise, the village slowly opened its eyes.

The first sound was the ringing of a temple bell. Roosters called from behind the houses, and smoke rose from kitchen chimneys as women prepared the morning meal.

The fragrance of freshly cooked rice mixed with the smell of wet earth, jasmine, and coconut leaves.

Women sprinkled water outside their houses and drew beautiful muggu patterns at their doorsteps. Men carried farming tools toward the fields, while boys cycled to school with books tied together by cotton string.

The village had its own clock.

The morning began with temple bells.

The afternoon belonged to the farmers and the shade of the trees.

The evening arrived with cattle returning home, children playing in the streets, and families gathering beneath their verandas.

When the sun disappeared behind the coconut trees, the entire village seemed to breathe more slowly.

The Godavari was the heart of the village.

Before sunrise, farmers walked along the narrow bunds between their paddy fields. Water flowed through irrigation channels, reflecting the pale orange sky. Egrets stood among the crops, and small wooden boats moved slowly through the canals.

Fishermen returned with their catch. Women carried brass pots near the water. Children sat on the riverbank, throwing little stones into the water and watching the ripples spread.

The river provided food, work, transport, and hope.

When the rains arrived on time, farmers celebrated. When the water was scarce, every family worried.

People depended on one another.

If a farmer fell ill during planting season, his neighbors helped him work the fields. If a family arranged a wedding, the entire street helped prepare food, decorate the courtyard, and welcome the guests.

When a child was born, women arrived with food and blessings. When someone died, neighbors came before being asked, bringing meals and whatever help the family needed.

People borrowed rice, tamarind, firewood, and small amounts of money from one another.

Children could be scolded by a neighbor for misbehaving and still be given a plate of food in that same neighbor’s house.

Yet the village had another side.

Everybody knew everybody.

A young man speaking repeatedly to an unmarried girl could become the subject of conversation before sunset. A rumor might travel from a well to a tea stall, from a tea stall to a neighbor’s courtyard, and from there to a girl’s father’s ears.

Many people cared deeply about the young, but some believed that protecting a family’s reputation required strict control over a daughter’s choices.

It was a world where kindness and judgment often lived on the same street.

And in that village lived Lakshmi.

She was seventeen, with long black hair, expressive eyes, and a smile that could brighten even the darkest afternoon.

Her father, Narayana Rao, was a respected schoolteacher. He believed education gave a child knowledge, but he also believed that discipline and family honor must guide every decision.

Lakshmi’s mother, Saraswati, was a gentle woman who loved her daughter but rarely challenged her husband’s authority.

Their ancestral house had red clay roof tiles, wooden doors, a central courtyard, and a tulasi plant beside the entrance. A brass lamp burned in the evenings, and the kitchen smelled of spices, fresh rice, and wood smoke.

Lakshmi helped her mother cook, drew rangoli every morning, and visited the local library to borrow books.

She dreamed of a life filled with small, beautiful things: a peaceful home, a garden of jasmine, children laughing in the courtyard, and someone who would understand her without forcing her to explain every feeling.

She did not know that the person she was dreaming of was already waiting for her in the library.

His name was Ravi.

He was twenty-two, the son of a modest farmer. His father had died when he was young, leaving Ravi to help his mother and younger sister.

He worked hard in the fields and took whatever opportunities he could find to support his family. He owned two pairs of good trousers, three shirts, a bicycle, and a watch that had belonged to his father.

He was not wealthy.

But he was patient, kind, and respectful.

He listened when older people spoke. He helped neighbors repair broken fences and carried heavy sacks for farmers who struggled with their work.

He had learned that a person’s worth was not measured by the size of their house, but by the goodness they carried inside it.

The first time he saw Lakshmi, she was standing inside the village library, trying to reach a book on the top shelf.

Ravi stepped forward.

“May I?”

Lakshmi moved aside.

He took down the book and handed it to her.

Their fingers touched for a brief moment.

Lakshmi lowered her eyes.

“Thank you,” she whispered.

Ravi smiled.

“You’re welcome.”

That was all.

But when Lakshmi walked home that evening, she remembered his smile.

And when Ravi reached home, he found himself wondering whether the girl with jasmine flowers in her hair would return the next day.

She did.

And the day after that.

Their meetings began quietly.

Lakshmi would arrive at the library, and Ravi would be reading near the window. She would borrow a book, and he would move aside to let her pass.

Sometimes, he found a fallen flower near her chair and placed it carefully on the windowsill.

Sometimes, she returned a book with a pressed flower hidden between its pages.

Neither mentioned it.

But both understood.

One afternoon, Lakshmi reached for a book and found a folded piece of paper inside it.

There were only four lines.

“The sky becomes beautiful when the sun begins to leave.

The garden becomes beautiful when flowers begin to bloom.

My day becomes beautiful when you enter this room.

I wonder if yours does too.”

Lakshmi read the lines twice.

Then she looked across the room.

Ravi was pretending to read a newspaper.

She tried to hide her smile.

He saw it.

And that smile became his favorite thing in the world.

Their love grew through the smallest moments.

When Lakshmi walked to the temple with her mother, Ravi sometimes passed on his bicycle. He never called her name or stopped her. He simply lowered his head respectfully.

Lakshmi would pretend to look at the temple flowers.

But as Ravi passed, her eyes followed him.

When Ravi worked in the fields during the hot afternoons, Lakshmi sometimes visited the well near the roadside. If she knew he would pass that way, she left a small brass tumbler beside it.

He would drink the water and leave the tumbler exactly where he found it.

The next morning, she would discover a fresh jasmine flower beside the tumbler.

Their entire world existed inside these little exchanges.

One afternoon, heavy rain surprised Lakshmi on her way home from the library.

She stood beneath a shop awning, holding her books against her chest.

Ravi arrived on his bicycle and stopped a little distance away.

“Your house is far,” he said.

She nodded.

“The rain may continue.”

She nodded again.

He looked at his bicycle, then at her books.

“I can walk with you. I’ll leave my bicycle at the shop.”

Lakshmi wanted to say yes.

But she remembered her father’s warnings.

She shook her head.

Ravi understood.

He placed his umbrella on the ground between them.

“Take it.”

“What about you?” she asked.

“I know the way home.”

She hesitated.

He smiled.

“Please. The books will get wet.”

Lakshmi picked up the umbrella.

She walked home alone, carrying his umbrella and trying not to smile too much.

Ravi walked beside his bicycle through the rain.

The next morning, Lakshmi returned the umbrella. She had dried it carefully and folded a little piece of paper around its handle.

“Thank you for caring about my books.”

Ravi kept that paper inside his watch case.

He never told her.

That year, during Deepavali, Lakshmi’s family decorated their courtyard with oil lamps.

Her mother dressed her in a green silk saree and placed jasmine flowers in her hair.

As Lakshmi stood before the mirror, her younger cousin teased her.

“Akka, why are you looking at yourself so much today?”

Lakshmi blushed.

“I’m checking whether my saree is properly arranged.”

Her cousin laughed.

“Of course. The saree.”

That evening, Ravi came to the street with his mother to deliver sweets to a neighbor.

He had no reason to pass Lakshmi’s house.

At least, that was what he told himself.

The courtyard was glowing with lamps.

Lakshmi stood near the entrance, holding a small plate of flowers.

For a moment, their eyes met across the street.

Ravi stopped walking.

Lakshmi forgot what she was holding.

The sounds of firecrackers seemed to disappear.

She wanted him to see her.

He wanted her to know that he had come.

But neither moved closer.

Lakshmi’s father noticed her looking toward the street.

“Inside,” he ordered.

She obeyed immediately.

Ravi lowered his eyes and continued walking.

That night, Lakshmi sat beside her bedroom window, watching the last of the lamps burn.

Her cousin came and sat beside her.

“Do you love him?”

Lakshmi’s eyes widened.

“Don’t say such things.”

“Then why do you look at him as if the whole world is standing inside his eyes?”

Lakshmi looked down.

After a long silence, she whispered, “Because I feel safe when he is near.”

Her cousin held her hand.

“Then I hope he always stays near.”

Lakshmi turned toward the window.

She did not know that one day, those words would become the prayer of her entire life.

Months passed.

Ravi’s mother began searching for a suitable bride for him.

One evening, she mentioned a girl from a nearby village.

“She comes from a good family,” his mother said. “Her parents have agreed to meet us next month.”

Ravi sat silently.

His mother noticed.

“Is there someone else?”

He lowered his eyes.

He had never spoken Lakshmi’s name inside his house.

“Amma, if I marry someone, I must be able to give her a good life.”

His mother nodded.

“That is why we must choose carefully.”

He wanted to tell her everything.

He wanted to say that there was a girl whose smile made even the hardest day bearable. A girl he had never held by the hand, never spoken to alone for more than a few minutes, and never asked to marry him.

Yet he had already imagined a lifetime beside her.

Instead, he said, “Please give me some time.”

His mother sighed.

“A man cannot wait forever, Ravi.”

He knew she was right.

The next afternoon, he went to the library.

Lakshmi was there.

He placed a book on the table in front of her.

Inside it was another letter.

“Tomorrow, the first monsoon rain may come.

I will wait beneath the banyan tree near your grandmother’s old house.

If you come, I will understand that your heart has chosen me.

If you do not come, I will never trouble you again.

I will remember you with respect, whatever happens.

— Ravi”

Lakshmi read the letter.

Her fingers tightened around the paper.

She looked at him.

He stood several feet away, looking at the floor.

For the first time, she wanted to break every rule she had ever followed.

She wanted to tell him that she loved him.

She wanted to say that she had already chosen him in her heart.

But the words remained trapped inside her.

She folded the letter and placed it inside her book.

Then she looked at Ravi.

She gave him the smallest nod.

He understood.

For the first time in his life, Ravi felt that tomorrow might bring him everything he had ever wanted.

That evening, Lakshmi returned home with the letter hidden inside her blouse.

Her father was waiting in the courtyard.

“Where were you?”

“At the library, Father.”

He held out his hand.

“Give me your books.”

Her heart stopped.

He opened them one by one.

When he found Ravi’s letter, his expression changed.

“Who is Ravi?”

Lakshmi stood motionless.

“Answer me!”

“He is—”

“Do not lie to me!”

Her mother came running from the kitchen.

Narayana Rao read the letter again.

“You will not see this boy again.”

“Father, please—”

“Silence!”

His voice filled the courtyard.

“Do you understand what people will say? A schoolteacher’s daughter meeting a young man without the family’s permission? Do you want to destroy your mother’s name and mine?”

Lakshmi began crying.

“I have done nothing wrong.”

“Then why is there a letter arranging a meeting?”

She had no answer.

Her father locked her inside her room.

The windows were secured from the outside, and the key was kept in his pocket.

“Tomorrow morning, we are leaving for your uncle’s village,” he announced. “You will stay there until your mind is clear.”

Lakshmi fell to her knees.

“Please, Father. I beg you. Don’t send me away.”

He turned his face.

“One day, you will understand that parents know what is best for their children.”

The door closed.

Lakshmi sat on the floor, holding Ravi’s letter against her chest.

She cried until her eyes hurt.

Outside, the wind began to blow.

Clouds gathered above the village.

The first monsoon rain was coming.

And beneath the old banyan tree, Ravi would be waiting.

Ravi reached the banyan tree before sunrise.

He wore his cleanest white shirt and carried a small bunch of jasmine flowers.

He had also brought a packet of sugar candies. Lakshmi had once told him that she loved them as a child.

He sat beneath the tree and watched the road.

The sky darkened.

The first drops of rain fell on the leaves.

Ravi stood up.

He looked toward the road.

No Lakshmi.

He waited.

An hour passed.

Then another.

The rain grew heavier.

Water ran down his face and soaked his shirt. The flowers in his hand began to fall apart.

Still, he waited.

Every time he heard footsteps, he turned.

Every time a cart passed, he stood.

Every time a young woman appeared in the distance, his heart leaped.

But it was never her.

By afternoon, the road had become a stream of muddy water.

Ravi sat against the trunk of the banyan tree.

He looked at the flowers.

“Maybe she couldn’t come,” he whispered.

He tried to smile.

“Maybe she is afraid.”

He remembered the way Lakshmi lowered her eyes whenever he looked at her. He remembered the umbrella, the flower, and the little note.

He could not believe that all those moments meant nothing.

But he had made a promise.

If she did not come, he would never trouble her again.

So he placed the remaining jasmine flowers near the tree roots.

He took out a piece of paper and wrote:

“Even if life takes us apart, I will remember this place.”

He underlined the sentence twice.

Then he folded the paper and slipped it into a blue envelope.

He would give it to her if he ever saw her again.

He walked home through the rain.

At the same time, Lakshmi was being taken to her uncle’s village in a bullock cart.

She pressed her face against the small opening in the cloth covering the cart.

The road to her uncle’s village passed near the banyan tree.

She saw the tree in the distance.

For one brief moment, she thought she saw a young man standing beneath it.

Her heart began to race.

“Stop!” she cried.

Her uncle’s servant turned around.

“What happened?”

“Please, stop the cart!”

But the servant kept walking.

“Your father has given strict instructions. We cannot stop.”

Lakshmi looked back.

The tree grew smaller.

The young man disappeared behind the rain.

She did not know whether she had truly seen Ravi.

She did not know that he had been waiting for her since sunrise.

She did not know that the flowers at the foot of the tree had been meant for her.

All she knew was that she had failed to keep the only promise she had ever wanted to make.

And for the rest of her life, she would remember the first monsoon rain.

Lakshmi returned to the village several months later.

Her father rarely mentioned Ravi.

Her mother avoided the subject.

Lakshmi went back to the library, but Ravi was never there.

She visited the well where she had left the brass tumbler.

She walked past the shop where he had once left his bicycle.

She looked toward the banyan tree whenever she passed that street.

Nothing.

No letters.

No flowers.

No smile from across the road.

One afternoon, she gathered the courage to ask the shopkeeper whether he had seen Ravi.

The old man looked uncomfortable.

“He has been busy with work,” he said.

It was a lie.

Ravi had not returned to the library because he believed Lakshmi had chosen not to come. He did not want to make her life difficult.

He accepted work at a distant agricultural cooperative and began helping his family with their debts.

He never married.

When his mother asked why, he said he was not ready.

Years later, when she asked again, he told her only this:

“Amma, not every person who remains unmarried is waiting for someone. Sometimes, a man simply learns to live with a part of his heart that will never be full.”

His mother did not ask again.

Meanwhile, Lakshmi’s father arranged her marriage to a kind and respectable man named Suryanarayana.

He was a clerk in a government office, a man who treated people gently and believed in family duty.

Lakshmi met him twice before the wedding.

He asked her whether she liked music.

She said yes.

He asked whether she enjoyed reading.

She said yes again.

Then he noticed her sad eyes.

“Are you unhappy about this marriage?” he asked.

Lakshmi looked at him in surprise.

She wanted to tell him the truth.

But she knew that he had done nothing wrong.

So she said, “I am afraid of leaving my parents.”

He smiled.

“That is natural. I will never ask you to stop loving them.”

For the first time, Lakshmi felt a little kindness toward the man she was going to marry.

Their wedding was arranged for the following month.

Lakshmi wore a red silk saree. Her mother placed jasmine flowers in her hair. Relatives filled the house, and the courtyard was decorated with mango leaves and colorful rangoli.

People laughed.

Priests chanted prayers.

The smell of ghee, incense, and fresh flowers filled the air.

Everyone said Lakshmi looked beautiful.

But inside her heart, a single question remained.

“Where are you, Ravi?”

She had never learned how to say goodbye to him.

She had never heard him say that he loved her.

And she had never known whether he had understood why she failed to come that day.

As the wedding ceremony began, Lakshmi looked once toward the entrance.

She imagined Ravi standing there.

She imagined him walking toward her and asking her to leave with him.

She imagined herself saying yes.

Then the priest called her attention back to the ceremony.

Her future was being decided.

Her past was standing silently beneath a banyan tree.

On the morning of Lakshmi’s wedding, Ravi woke before sunrise.

He had received no invitation. He did not know the exact date of her marriage, but he had heard from a distant acquaintance that her family had arranged a match.

For several days, he had been unable to sleep.

He wanted to go to her house.

He wanted to see her once.

He wanted to ask why she had never come.

But he remembered his promise.

He would never trouble her.

That morning, he walked to the old banyan tree.

He carried a single jasmine flower.

The tree had grown wider, and its roots had spread across the ground. Rainwater still collected in a hollow near its trunk.

Ravi sat beneath it.

“I hope you are happy, Lakshmi,” he whispered.

He took the blue envelope from his pocket.

It was the letter he had written on the day she failed to come.

He had carried it with him for months, hoping that one day he might give it to her.

He placed the envelope inside an old book and tucked the book beneath a flat stone near the tree’s roots.

He had no intention of returning for it.

Then he stood, brushed the dust from his trousers, and started walking toward the main road.

That afternoon, Ravi was traveling to a nearby village on his bicycle to deliver medicine to his sick mother.

At a narrow crossing outside town, a heavily loaded lorry came around a bend.

The driver tried to stop, but the road was slippery from the monsoon rain.

Ravi saw the lorry.

He tried to move aside.

There was a terrible crash.

Villagers rushed toward the road.

Someone ran to fetch a doctor.

Someone else searched Ravi’s pockets for his family details.

By evening, Ravi was gone.

He died on the same day Lakshmi became a bride.

No one from Lakshmi’s family was there.

No one knew that the boy who had loved her so quietly had carried her name in his heart until his final breath.

His mother received the news that evening.

She sat beside his body and held his hand.

“My son,” she cried, “you never told me whom you were waiting for.”

But Ravi could no longer answer.

In his room, his mother found a watch, a few old books, and a small folded paper.

It contained four lines:

“The sky becomes beautiful when the sun begins to leave.

The garden becomes beautiful when flowers begin to bloom.

My day becomes beautiful when you enter this room.

I wonder if yours does too.”

His mother did not know who had written the lines.

She placed the paper inside his watch case.

Then she closed the box.

And with it, she closed the final door to a love story that had never been told.

Years passed.

Lakshmi became a wife, then a mother, and eventually a grandmother.

Suryanarayana was a good man.

He cared for her when she was ill. He brought home sweets for their children. He listened when she spoke and never raised his hand against her.

Lakshmi respected him deeply.

She fulfilled her responsibilities as a wife and mother. She cooked for her family, celebrated festivals, raised her children, and welcomed grandchildren into the world.

She learned to laugh again.

She learned to smile without feeling guilty.

She learned that a person could live a meaningful life even while carrying a memory that never faded.

But every year, when the first monsoon rain arrived, she became quiet.

She would stand beside the window and look toward the banyan tree.

Sometimes, she placed fresh jasmine flowers near the windowsill.

Sometimes, she opened an old book and read a few lines.

She never told her children why.

Her husband noticed her sadness one evening.

“Do you miss your childhood?” he asked.

Lakshmi looked at him.

“Sometimes.”

He nodded.

“Then keep those memories. They are part of you.”

She felt tears gather in her eyes.

He did not ask another question.

He never knew that the sadness in her heart belonged to someone she had loved before she became his wife.

And Lakshmi never knew that Ravi had died on the very day she had entered her marriage.

She believed he had gone somewhere far away.

Perhaps he had married.

Perhaps he had forgotten her.

Perhaps, she sometimes thought, he had hated her for breaking their promise.

That last thought hurt the most.

She wished she could have told him the truth.

She wished she could have explained that she had waited for him too, behind a locked door, crying until the rain stopped.

But life had moved forward.

Her children grew up.

Her husband grew old.

And eventually, Suryanarayana passed away peacefully after a long life.

Lakshmi mourned him with genuine sorrow.

She had lost a companion, a protector, and a man who had treated her with kindness.

She continued living in their old house, surrounded by photographs, memories, and the quiet footsteps of grandchildren who visited whenever they could.

She had everything she needed.

Yet somewhere in the deepest corner of her heart, the girl beneath the banyan tree was still waiting.

She was still seventeen.

Still holding a blue envelope.

Still looking down the road.

Still hoping a young man in a white shirt would appear through the rain.

When Lakshmi finished telling the story, Meera sat perfectly still.

She had never seen her grandmother cry like that.

“Ammaamma,” she whispered, “you loved him so much.”

Lakshmi smiled sadly.

“We were young.”

“But why didn’t you search for him? Why didn’t you ask anyone?”

Lakshmi looked toward the banyan tree.

“At that time, a girl’s life was not always her own to decide. I was afraid of my father. I was afraid of hurting my mother. And after I married, I thought Ravi had moved on.”

“But you never forgot him.”

“No,” Lakshmi whispered. “Some people leave your life. But the place they hold in your heart remains empty in a way no one else can fill.”

Meera held her grandmother’s hand.

“What happened to him?”

Lakshmi shook her head.

“I don’t know. I never found out.”

“Did he ever marry?”

“I don’t know, child.”

“Did he hate you?”

At that question, Lakshmi’s eyes filled with tears.

“That is what I have wondered for forty-six years.”

Meera looked at the letter again.

The handwriting was clear.

The envelope was old.

But the words were still alive.

She thought about Vijay.

She thought about the morning at the bus stop, his smile, the chocolates, the flowers, and the little gifts he had placed in her bag.

Suddenly, she felt ashamed.

Vijay had made a mistake during their argument. He had worried about her, but he had expressed himself badly. She had felt hurt, but instead of explaining her feelings, she had punished him with silence.

What if he misunderstood?

What if he stopped trying?

What if life took them in different directions before she found the courage to explain?

For the first time that day, Meera understood that silence could protect a person from a difficult conversation.

It could also destroy something beautiful.

She stood up.

“Ammaamma, I’m going to find him.”

Lakshmi looked confused.

“Find whom?”

“Ravi. We need to know what happened to him. You deserve to know.”

The old woman’s hands began to tremble.

“Meera, after all these years—”

“Forty-six years is a long time. But it is not too late to know the truth.”

Meera folded the letter and placed it safely in her bag.

“I’ll come back soon.”

Before leaving, she sent Vijay a message.

“I’m sorry I hurt you today. There is something important I need to tell you, but I need a little time. Please don’t think I have stopped loving you.”

Vijay read the message twice.

He didn’t understand what had happened, but relief washed over him.

He replied:

“Take the time you need. I’ll be here when you’re ready to talk.”

Meera looked at his reply and held the phone close to her heart.

Then she left to search for Ravi.

The next morning, she visited the old library.

It was no longer the small building she remembered from childhood. The shelves had been replaced, the windows had been painted, and the old wooden tables were gone.

But an elderly librarian still worked there.

When Meera showed him the letter, he adjusted his spectacles and studied it carefully.

“Ravi?” he said. “There were several young men by that name.”

“His father’s name may have been—”

She stopped.

Her grandmother had never told her.

The librarian examined the handwriting.

“I remember an old family living near the agricultural cooperative. The son was a quiet young man. His mother used to visit this library sometimes.”

“Do you remember his full name?”

The old man thought for a long time.

“Ravi Kumar, perhaps. I cannot be certain.”

He opened an old register and searched through its yellowing pages.

After several minutes, he found an entry from 1974.

A young man named Ravi Kumar had borrowed a book on agricultural methods.

The address was in a nearby village.

Meera copied it carefully.

That afternoon, she traveled there.

The village had changed. Roads had been widened, houses had been rebuilt, and the fields had been divided among new owners.

She asked an elderly shopkeeper about Ravi Kumar.

The man looked at her for a long time.

“Are you related to him?”

“No. I’m trying to find out what happened to him.”

The shopkeeper’s expression softened.

“Ravi was a good boy. He died very young.”

Meera felt the world stop.

“When?”

“Many years ago. In an accident. I believe it happened in 1974.”

“What day?”

The shopkeeper could not remember.

He directed her to an old woman who lived near the village temple.

She was Ravi’s younger sister.

Her name was Kamala.

When Meera showed her the letter, Kamala began to cry.

“I have not seen this handwriting in so many years.”

“Did Ravi love someone named Lakshmi?”

Kamala stared at her.

“How do you know that name?”

Meera explained everything.

Kamala listened without interrupting.

When Meera finished, the old woman covered her face with her hands.

“My brother never told us much. But he had a book he treasured. He kept a folded paper inside it. After he died, my mother found that paper in his room.”

Meera’s heart raced.

“What happened to it?”

“I have it.”

Kamala disappeared into the next room and returned with a small wooden box.

Inside lay an old watch case.

She opened it.

The folded paper was still there.

Meera unfolded it carefully.

It was the same poem about the sky, the garden, and the girl who made his day beautiful.

At the bottom, Ravi had written a name.

“Lakshmi.”

Meera began to cry.

He had loved her.

He had remembered her.

And he had never forgotten her.

“Do you know when he died?” Meera asked.

Kamala nodded.

“It was the day he died that my mother told me he had been waiting for a woman. We never knew her full story. He died on the day of her wedding, though we didn’t know that at the time.”

Meera could barely breathe.

“What date?”

Kamala told her.

It was the same date as Lakshmi’s wedding.

For a moment, Meera could not speak.

The two people who had once stood on opposite sides of the same impossible love story had spent their final moments in entirely different worlds.

Lakshmi had been surrounded by wedding music.

Ravi had died on a rain-soaked road.

She had thought he had forgotten her.

He had died believing she had chosen to leave him behind.

Neither had known the truth.

Meera pressed the paper against her chest.

“Ammaamma must know.”

She thanked Kamala and hurried back to town.

The sky was cloudy when Meera returned.

She carried the blue envelope, the old poem, and the truth that had remained hidden for forty-six years.

She imagined her grandmother smiling when she heard the news.

She imagined telling her that Ravi had loved her until his last day.

She imagined the tears of relief that would finally wash away decades of uncertainty.

When she reached the house, the front door was open.

“Ammaamma!” she called.

There was no answer.

She walked into the courtyard.

Her grandmother was sitting in her favorite chair near the window, wearing a pale blue saree.

The same color as the envelope.

“Ammaamma, I found him!”

Lakshmi slowly turned her head.

Meera knelt beside her.

“I found Ravi’s sister. He never married. He never forgot you. He kept your name with him until the day he died.”

Lakshmi’s lips parted.

For several seconds, she simply stared at Meera.

Then a tear slipped down her cheek.

“He remembered me?”

“Yes, Ammaamma. He remembered you.”

Meera took the old poem and placed it in her grandmother’s hands.

“He died on the day of your wedding.”

Lakshmi looked at the paper.

Her fingers trembled.

She read her name.

Then she read the poem again.

A sound escaped her throat, somewhere between a sob and a laugh.

“He didn’t hate me?”

“No, Ammaamma.”

“He knew I loved him?”

Meera’s eyes filled with tears.

“I don’t know whether he knew everything. But he never stopped loving you.”

Lakshmi closed her eyes.

For the first time in forty-six years, the question that had haunted her finally had an answer.

She had not been forgotten.

Her love had not been a foolish childhood dream.

Somewhere in the world, there had been another heart that remembered her exactly as she remembered him.

Lakshmi pressed the paper against her chest.

“Ravi,” she whispered.

Meera wrapped her arms around her grandmother.

“I wish I could have brought him to you.”

Lakshmi smiled through her tears.

“No, child. You brought me something more precious.”

“What?”

“The truth.”

Outside, the wind began to blow.

The first drops of rain touched the window.

Lakshmi looked toward the banyan tree.

For one brief moment, Meera thought her grandmother’s face had changed. The deep lines of age seemed softer. Her eyes looked young again.

Lakshmi whispered something Meera could barely hear.

“I am coming.”

Then she became very still.

“Ammaamma?”

Meera gently shook her hand.

“Ammaamma, look at me.”

There was no answer.

The old woman’s fingers loosened around the paper.

Her head rested against the chair.

Meera touched her grandmother’s cheek.

It was still warm.

But the woman who had carried a secret for nearly half a century had taken her final breath.

“Ammaamma!”

Meera cried out.

She held Lakshmi in her arms and begged her to open her eyes.

But the rain continued falling.

And the banyan tree stood silently outside the window.

The old woman had waited forty-six years to learn whether the man she loved had remembered her.

She had received her answer.

And then, as if her heart had finally found peace, she had left the world.

The days after Lakshmi’s death passed like a dream.

Relatives came to the house. Neighbors offered condolences. The courtyard filled with familiar faces, but Meera barely heard their voices.

She kept the blue envelope beside her grandmother’s photograph.

At the funeral, she placed fresh jasmine flowers near Lakshmi’s hands.

She remembered the girl who had once stood beside the library window.

She remembered the young woman who had been locked inside her room while the monsoon rain fell outside.

She remembered the bride who had looked toward the entrance, hoping to see a familiar face.

And she remembered the grandmother who had finally learned that she had been loved all her life, even when she believed herself forgotten.

After the funeral, Meera returned to the banyan tree.

She carried the blue envelope, the poem, and two small bunches of jasmine.

The rain had stopped.

Water dripped slowly from the leaves.

She knelt near the roots and placed the flowers on the ground.

“Ravi,” she whispered, “she remembered you every day.”

Then she looked up toward the branches.

“And Lakshmi, I hope you finally found him.”

The wind moved through the leaves.

For a moment, Meera thought she heard a gentle sound, almost like someone laughing in the distance.

Perhaps it was only the wind.

Perhaps it was only a memory.

Or perhaps, somewhere beyond the world she knew, two young hearts had finally found their way back to each other.

A few weeks later, Meera returned to college.

She found Vijay waiting at the same bus stop.

He wore the blue shirt she had always liked.

In his hand was a small bunch of fresh jasmine flowers.

When he saw her, he stood up.

For a moment, neither spoke.

Meera walked toward him.

Vijay looked at her uncertainly.

“Are you still angry with me?”

She shook her head.

“I’m sorry.”

He blinked.

“For what?”

“For making you feel invisible.”

His expression softened.

“I thought you needed time.”

“I was afraid,” she whispered. “And instead of telling you, I made you suffer.”

Vijay looked down at the flowers.

“I bought these yesterday. I was going to give them to you today.”

Meera took them gently.

Then she held his hand.

“My grandmother spent her whole life wondering whether the man she loved had forgotten her. I don’t want us to lose years because we are too proud to speak.”

Vijay looked into her eyes.

For the first time, he understood that the girl standing before him was carrying a grief much larger than their argument.

He held her hand a little more tightly.

“Then let’s promise each other something.”

“What?”

“If we are angry, we will talk. If we are afraid, we will tell each other. If life becomes difficult, we will face it together.”

Meera smiled through her tears.

“And if we ever lose our way?”

Vijay looked toward the old banyan tree.

“Then we will find each other again.”

Meera rested her head against his shoulder.

The first drops of rain began to fall.

They stood beneath the banyan tree, holding hands, while the town moved around them.

Somewhere in the distance, a temple bell rang.

The jasmine flowers in Meera’s hand released their sweet fragrance.

And for the first time in many days, her heart felt light.

She could not change what had happened to her grandmother.

She could not give Lakshmi the years she had lost.

But she could carry her story forward.

She could remember the love that had lived without promises, survived without answers, and waited beyond the reach of time.

That evening, Meera placed the blue envelope inside a new book.

On its first page, she wrote:

“For every heart that has loved silently.

For every person who has waited.

For every soul that believes love is more than words.”

Then she closed the book.

Outside her window, the rain continued falling.

And beneath the old banyan tree, two bunches of jasmine rested side by side.

One for Lakshmi.

One for Ravi.

Two lives that had never been allowed to become one.

Two hearts that had never stopped remembering.

And one promise that time could never erase.

TRUE LOVE NEVER DIES

Some people are lucky enough to spend their entire lives with the person they love.

Others carry that person in their hearts until their final breath.

Lakshmi and Ravi never shared a home. They never stood together at their wedding. They never had the chance to grow old beside each other.

But their love survived every silence, every separation, and every year that passed between them.

Perhaps, when Lakshmi closed her eyes for the final time, she no longer saw the old house or the banyan tree.

Perhaps she saw a young man in a white shirt, standing beneath the first monsoon rain, holding a bunch of jasmine flowers.

Perhaps he smiled and asked her the question he had waited a lifetime to ask.

“Why didn’t you come that day, Lakshmi?”

And perhaps she finally answered.

“I tried, Ravi. I tried to come to you.”

Perhaps he understood.

Perhaps he held out his hand.

And perhaps, after forty-six years of waiting, she finally placed her hand in his.

TRUE LOVE NEVER DIES.

Somewhere beyond this world, may Lakshmi and Ravi finally be together.

May they stand beneath their banyan tree, listen to the monsoon rain, and live the love that life never allowed them to finish.

Some love stories end with a wedding. Some end with a goodbye. And some wait until eternity to begin again.
