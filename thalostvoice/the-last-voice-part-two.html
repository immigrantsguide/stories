AN ORIGINAL CRIME THRILLER · PART TWO

THE LAST VOICE

THE PRICE OF GREED

They thought they had purchased death. They never imagined death would come looking for them.

Created by Vijay Krishna

Ravi had not spoken to anyone for eleven days. He was in judicial custody following the death of Arjun inside the prison. Arjun had survived the earlier confrontation but had suffered serious injuries and was being held separately while the authorities investigated. Ravi had lost his wife, his home, his savings and almost every reason to live. He kept one thing with him: Sruthi's photograph.

Every morning, he looked at it. Every night, he remembered the same sentence: “I just wanted one last hug.”

One afternoon, a prison officer told him that Vikram Varma had come to visit. Vikram entered the visitors' room looking older and exhausted. He placed a thick file on the table.

“I found out who was sitting beside my son that afternoon,” Vikram said.

“Who?” Ravi asked.

“Her name is Dr. Nandita Rao. She was associated with a private transplant coordination network. She is now the person who controls the hospital auctions.”

Vikram opened the file. There were photographs of a woman wearing a dark blue dress and sitting in the front passenger seat of Arjun's BMW. Ravi recognised her from the recovered footage.

“She was there,” he whispered.

“She wasn't an ordinary passenger,” Vikram replied. “She planned everything.”

“Why Sruthi?”

Vikram took a long breath. “Because she needed her.”

Nandita Rao was not an ordinary criminal. She had built a private network connecting wealthy families, corrupt medical administrators, middlemen and selected hospital employees. Her public identity was that of a wealthy medical philanthropist who supported transplant patients. Her private business was something else: the sale of final patient recordings and an illegal organ-trafficking operation.

Her clients included billionaires, influential businessmen, politicians, wealthy heirs and their children. Some wanted to hear the last words of strangers. Some wanted private confessions. Some wanted to hear people confess their greed, anger, hatred and secrets. Others paid enormous amounts for recordings containing unusual statements about demons, angels, death, God, betrayal and things people claimed to have seen before dying.

For these buyers, a dying person's final words were entertainment, a private obsession or a means of obtaining compromising information. Nandita had turned human suffering into a luxury business.

She personally contacted wealthy clients and their children. Her messages promised confidential services if a family member faced a serious medical emergency, accident or unexpected complication. Some clients wanted recordings. Others wanted medical favours. A few wanted something far more sinister. Nandita arranged everything, and Mahesh, the hospital administrator, was one of her most valuable employees.

But Sruthi was different.

Sruthi had donated blood several times. One of those donations had taken place on Ravi's birthday. Every year, Ravi celebrated his birthday by helping organise a small blood donation drive. Sruthi joined him because she knew it made him happy. To her, donating blood was simply a way to help someone.

During one of those donation drives, her blood group and medical information entered a confidential medical record. Through corrupt access to medical databases, Nandita obtained that information. Sruthi had a rare blood group and an unusually valuable tissue-compatibility profile. Nandita was herself waiting for a transplant. Her condition had become increasingly serious, and her private doctors had told her that finding a suitable donor was extremely difficult.

She saw Sruthi's medical profile: age, blood group, tissue compatibility and medical history. To Nandita, Sruthi was no longer a person. She was an opportunity.

For two weeks, Nandita's people followed Sruthi. They learned where she lived, where she shopped, when she travelled and where Ravi worked. They learned about their marriage and the fifteen days of silence. They even knew that Sruthi regularly rode her scooter along the L.B. Nagar road.

Nandita decided that an accident would be the easiest way to make Sruthi disappear. She arranged for Arjun to drive the BMW. She sat beside him. She had already arranged the people who would take Sruthi to the hospital. Mahesh had prepared the hospital side of the operation.

The accident was not an accident.

The hospital staff were instructed to falsify records, conceal the criminal involvement and manipulate the medical process. The transplant operation was conducted through an illegal network, without Sruthi's consent or her family's authorisation. The hospital declared Sruthi dead. Ravi was left with a final bill. Nandita received what she had wanted.

But she made one mistake. She allowed Sruthi's final words to be recorded. She believed that even if the recording contained something dangerous, she could buy it, edit it or destroy it. She never imagined that those words would eventually reach the man whose life she had destroyed.

Vikram explained everything to Ravi. “Your wife was not simply involved in an accident. She was selected. She was followed for two weeks. They knew about her blood donation. They knew her medical compatibility. They knew everything.”

Ravi pressed his hands against his face. “She donated blood on my birthday.”

The memory returned: Sruthi standing beside him at the blood donation camp, smiling and asking how much blood they would take. She had joked that if she became weak, he would have to buy her ice cream. She had even offered to donate twice. He remembered laughing with her that day. Now he understood that something she had done to save another person's life had been used to select her for death.

Ravi began crying. “She saved someone else's life with her blood... and they killed her for it.”

Vikram couldn't answer. Ravi slowly pushed the file away. “Tell me where she is.”

“Who?”

“That woman.”

“She's still operating the network,” Vikram said.

Ravi stood up. “Then she is not finished.”

Nandita had become increasingly powerful. Following Sruthi's death, the hospital auction business had expanded. Wealthy buyers were waiting for new recordings. One billionaire wanted to hear the confession of a man who had died after betraying his business partner. Another wanted a recording from a patient who repeatedly claimed to have seen a dark figure beside his bed. A wealthy young heir had paid an enormous amount for the final words of a woman who had refused to forgive her family.

Some buyers listened once. Others listened repeatedly. One buyer had an entire private collection. Nandita called them her special clients. She had turned the last moments of human beings into a private entertainment industry.

At one private gathering, a billionaire asked, “How do you find these recordings?”

Nandita calmly replied, “People die every day. Not everyone gets remembered. We simply preserve what they leave behind.”

“And how much?”

Nandita gave him a figure. He agreed without asking another question.

Nobody asked whether the patients had agreed to be recorded. Nobody asked whether their families knew. Nobody asked whether someone had been killed to obtain what they were buying. Money had removed the need for those questions.

Ravi's lawyer eventually secured bail in the hospital assault case, subject to the court's conditions. His other proceedings continued, and the investigation into Arjun's injuries remained pending. Ravi returned home. The house was exactly as he had left it. Sruthi's clothes were still in the cupboard. Her hairbrush was on the dressing table. Her small sandals were near the bedroom door.

The lunchbox was still there. Ravi opened the first compartment. The note was inside. He read it again. This time, he reached the last line: “Tonight, no fighting. Only hugs. Deal?”

He folded the paper carefully and looked at their wedding photograph.

“Sruthi, I found them,” he whispered. “I found the people who took you away.”

He put the photograph into his shirt pocket. He had one destination: Nandita Rao.

Nandita was staying at a private property outside Hyderabad. The property had a large security team, private offices and a separate medical wing. That evening, a group of wealthy clients had gathered for a private auction. The catalogue contained twenty recordings. One was listed as “LOT 019 — FEMALE PATIENT — FINAL CONFESSION.” Another was “LOT 020 — MALE PATIENT — UNUSUAL LAST STATEMENTS.”

Nandita stood at the front of the room, wearing a black dress and holding a tablet. “Welcome, everyone. Tonight's recordings have been authenticated. The original files are available to the highest bidder.”

Before she could continue, a guard entered. “Madam, there is a problem.”

“What?”

“Someone has entered the property.”

“Who?”

The guard hesitated. “Ravi.”

Nandita stopped smiling. She knew exactly who Ravi was. She knew about his wife, the accident and the hospital. She knew that he was the one person she had failed to silence.

Ravi entered the building. The security guards tried to stop him. He fought through them. He was injured, exhausted and consumed by grief, but he continued towards the auction hall. Nandita stood waiting.

“Ravi,” she said.

He stopped and looked at her.

“Did you know her?”

Nandita said nothing.

“Did you know my wife?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know she was waiting for me to come home?”

Nandita looked away.

“Did you know she made my favourite food that morning? Did you know she was calling me while your people were taking her to the hospital? Did you know she was asking for one last hug?”

Ravi's voice began breaking. Nandita finally answered, “Your wife was a compatible donor. Her profile was valuable. The opportunity came, and I took it.”

Ravi stared at her. “Opportunity?”

“People pay enormous amounts for what they need. I simply connected the supply and demand.”

Ravi's eyes filled with tears. “She was not supply.”

Nandita remained silent.

“She was not an organ. She was my wife.”

Nandita tried to defend herself. “Do you know how many people are waiting for organs? Do you know how much money is involved? This is a business. Everyone benefits.”

Ravi looked at the wealthy people sitting in the auction hall.

“Does everyone benefit?”

Nobody answered.

“Does the husband benefit when his wife never comes home? Does the child benefit when their mother is taken away? Does anyone here know what it feels like to hear the last words of someone you love being sold to strangers?”

Nobody moved.

Ravi turned towards Nandita. “You took her blood. You took her life. You took her voice. What else do you want?”

Nandita looked at him without remorse. “Money never stops, Ravi.”

That was the moment he lost control. A violent struggle followed. Ravi attacked Nandita. Security tried to intervene, but he continued fighting. In the chaos, Nandita suffered fatal injuries.

Ravi then destroyed the recording devices and smashed the auction equipment. He found the money kept for the private auction: bundles of currency, crores of rupees earned from the suffering of hundreds of families. He threw it into the room and set it on fire. The recordings burned. The money burned. The auction catalogue burned. The people who had paid to listen to strangers' last words ran towards the exits.

Ravi stood among the destruction. He was not celebrating. He was crying.

“Sruthi... it's over.”

But it wasn't over.

The police arrived. They found Nandita dead, the destroyed auction room and Ravi. He did not run or resist. When an officer approached him, Ravi held out his hands.

“Take me.”

The officer placed handcuffs on him. Ravi looked at the burning money.

“At least this money cannot buy another person's life.”

The investigation that followed exposed one of the most disturbing criminal networks the country had seen. The police recovered backup servers, financial records, medical documents and communications between Nandita, Mahesh and several wealthy clients. The destroyed auction room had not destroyed the business. Its records existed in other places.

Several hospitals were investigated. Several wealthy clients were questioned. Mahesh agreed to cooperate with investigators in exchange for consideration in his own case. His statements revealed how long the operation had existed and how many families had been affected.

The investigators also recovered evidence of Sruthi's illegal organ procurement. Vikram provided his documents and recordings. Suresh's dashcam footage established the BMW's involvement. The original audio established that Sruthi had identified Arjun and another passenger.

Arjun survived his injuries and was eventually convicted for his involvement in the fatal accident and conspiracy. His father's wealth could no longer protect him. Vikram was convicted for his own criminal involvement.

Ravi was charged with the murder of Nandita, along with the other offences committed during his revenge attack. He never denied what he had done.

During his trial, the prosecutor asked him why he had killed her.

“Because she took my wife and treated her like something she could purchase,” Ravi answered.

“Does that give you the right to take another person's life?”

Ravi remained silent for a long time. Then he answered, “No. It doesn't.”

He looked down. “But I wanted her to understand what it means when someone never comes home.”

The court eventually convicted him of murder and, in the fictional proceedings of this story, awarded him the death penalty by hanging, subject to the required higher-court confirmation and appeals. Ravi did not ask for sympathy. He asked for one thing: permission to keep Sruthi's photograph.

Vikram visited Ravi one final time. He brought a printed copy of the original recording.

“Ravi, I don't know whether you will forgive me.”

“Don't ask me to forgive you,” Ravi replied.

Vikram nodded. “I destroyed my own family trying to protect my son. You destroyed your future trying to avenge your wife. Arjun destroyed his life because he believed money would protect him.”

Ravi looked at the photograph. “We all lost something.”

Vikram began crying. “Your wife wanted to live. She wanted to go home. She wanted to hug you. I wish I had never bought that recording.”

Ravi answered quietly, “I wish I had answered her phone.”

Neither man spoke again.

On the final day of Ravi's appeal proceedings, he was asked whether he had anything to say. He requested permission to read a letter. It was the same letter Sruthi had placed inside his lunchbox.

He read it slowly. When he reached the words “I would still choose you,” his voice broke. He folded the letter and said:

“I spent fifteen days refusing to speak to the person who loved me most. I thought I had time. I thought tomorrow would come. I thought I could apologise later. I was wrong.

“Money took everything from us. Greed took her life. My anger took away what remained of mine.

“If there is one thing I want people to understand, it is this: Don't wait until someone's voice becomes a recording to realise how much it means to you.”

He returned the letter to his pocket.

Somewhere else, in another city, another private hospital was crowded with patients. It was 2:40 in the morning. A young man named Kiran was sitting outside the ICU. His wife, Anjali, had suffered a serious accident. He had already paid the emergency deposit. His savings were almost finished.

A hospital administrator approached him.

“Sir, there is a financial assistance programme. We can help you with the outstanding amount.”

Kiran looked relieved. “Please save my wife. I will arrange whatever is required.”

The administrator gave him a form. Kiran signed it without reading every line.

Inside a private office, another employee opened a computer. A new patient profile appeared:

PATIENT: ANJALI
BLOOD GROUP: RARE
FINANCIAL STATUS: CRITICAL
RECORDING STATUS: PENDING

A message arrived on the administrator's phone: NEW PRIVATE CLIENT REQUEST.

The administrator opened it.

“We are looking for a particular final recording. Budget is not a problem.”

He looked towards the ICU. Then he looked at the patient profile. He quietly opened another folder.

PATIENT RECORDINGS — NEW AUCTION

A new entry was created.

Far away, another wealthy man received a message: “We have something special for you.”

The business had not ended. It had only changed its location.

Another hospital. Another family. Another husband. Another wife. Another unpaid bill. And another person who believed that tomorrow would give him enough time to say everything he wanted to say.

THE FINAL MESSAGE

Ravi wanted to save Sruthi. Arjun wanted to escape responsibility. Vikram wanted to protect his son. Nandita wanted money, power and a new life. Mahesh wanted wealth. The buyers wanted entertainment, secrets and things they could never understand.

Every one of them believed money could solve their problems.

But money could not bring Sruthi home. It could not erase Ravi's guilt. It could not give Arjun another life. It could not restore Vikram's family. And it could not make Nandita's greed disappear.

Ravi became an example of what happens when grief turns into revenge.

Arjun became an example of what happens when privilege destroys responsibility.

Vikram became an example of what happens when a parent protects wrongdoing instead of correcting it.

Nandita became an example of what happens when human beings become numbers on a balance sheet.

And the hospital became an example of what happens when the need for money destroys the purpose of saving lives.

There is nothing wrong with earning money. There is nothing wrong with wanting a better life. There is nothing wrong with needing help when life becomes difficult.

But when greed becomes greater than humanity, when money becomes more valuable than a human life, and when someone's desperation becomes another person's business, society loses something that no amount of money can replace.

A life.
A family.
A voice.
A final hug.

And somewhere, in another hospital, another Ravi was waiting for his Sruthi. He still believed she would come home. He had no idea that someone had already put a price on her life.

THE END

Some people die once. Their families live through that death every day.
